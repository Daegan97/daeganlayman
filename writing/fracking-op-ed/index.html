Legal Loopholes and Confidentiality in the Fracking Industry

Written 2019. 4 min read.

Federal exemptions, toxic-substance law and state trade-secret law together let fracking companies keep the chemistry of their fluids secret. This op-ed lays out how that loophole works and three ways to close it.

Written in 2019 for an environmental studies course. Laws and figures are as they stood then.

How can one measure the environmental effects of the chemical substances used in fracking if we are constantly left in the dark about their composition and volume? There exists a loophole of sorts in the conjunction of federal laws concerning toxic substances and water usage with state level trade secret laws that ultimately allow for fracking companies to keep the chemical makeup of their hydraulic fracking fluid a trade secret and thus evade efforts to conduct health studies. While states can partially regulate fracking (in fact, over half of them do!), none are capable of forcing the companies to fully disclose the chemicals they use during their respective fracking processes. In order to adequately comprehend the health effects of fracking substances and the ramifications of their potential contamination of public or private water supplies, one or more of these legal protections must be revised or remanded so that fracking companies are forced to disclose information necessary to ensure the safety of the environment and its inhabitants.

As of 2005, in accordance with the Energy Policy Act, the federal government is barred from enacting any sort of regulations on fracking companies. Because of this, the regulation of fracking companies is wholly a state issue, which is problematic in the sense that even states like California with comparatively comprehensive regulatory measures in place obviously have no means of subjecting fracking companies operating outside of state lines to those same standards. What exists in fact is a fragmented and subpar regulatory framework across the nation that allows for these companies to remain relatively undisturbed in their day-to-day operations. Even the harshest laws do not force these companies to fully disclose the makeup of their fracturing fluid and most of the time, chemicals that are of concern are neglected by the Environmental Protection Agency or their state-level equivalents. If the language in the 2005 Energy Policy Act concerning the exemption of fracking companies from federal regulation was revised or redacted in some shape or form, then the federal government could attempt to implement regulatory measures that supersede the disparate state laws and allow for things like full disclosure and access to the chemicals previously hidden so that they can be studied and understood in the context of environmental and public health. As the Energy Policy Act stands now, the federal government has no standing to interfere with the fracking industry in a regulatory manner, as made evident by former President Obama’s unsuccessful attempt to establish fracking regulations through the United States Department of the Interior in 2016. A change to the Energy Policy Act would most likely allow for the most substantive changes to the status quo and allow for the dissipation of secrecy that clouds the fracking industry, but it is not the only change that could make a big difference.

Another avenue of approach for regulating the fracking industry is by addressing the chemicals they use in their fracking fluid under the federal Toxic Substances Control Act (TSCA) of 1976. While several disclosed fracking fluid chemicals have been identified as harmful under the TSCA, the law allows for the fracking industry to dodge disclosure of information and health testing through its lack of teeth and allowances for the industry to assert confidentiality in the name of trade secrecy in an alarming number of cases. If the TSCA was amended to purposefully neglect the fracking industry’s desire for trade secrecy and required health testing on those chemicals disclosed under that amendment, it would be a great step towards eroding the unknown element of this issue. Unfortunately, an amendment like that would likely be struck down with a citation of the Energy Policy Act’s exemption for fracking industries, so this point is largely moot without the amending and revision of the Energy Policy Act.

Similarly to how the TSCA allows for confidentiality, most states have fairly comprehensive trade secret laws that allow for companies to keep the ingredient composition of their respective products under lock and key so as to avoid competitors stealing their recipes and in turn indirectly profiting off of them. This is yet another outlet for fracking industries, who are subject to these laws, to remain capable of keeping their chemicals off the radar. A way states could circumvent this issue is by exempting fracking industries from the protections granted by their respective trade secret laws. In fact, forcing these companies to file for patents would allow for concerns about competitiveness to be alleviated since patent law provides more protection and security for the companies and their patented inventions than trade secret law while simultaneously acting as a means to urge them to disclose the chemical composition of their fracking fluids. Unfortunately, this would likely have to be instituted on a state-by-state level for the same reasons given for difficulties the federal government would encounter attempting to amend the TSCA. This would likely lead to great inconsistency from state to state, but would be a step in the right direction nonetheless.

Many arguments made for the sake of providing defense to fracking companies center on the unknown and assert that those companies remain unfettered until more is known about their effects on the environment. As demonstrated above, this logic is problematic because one can never truly hope to understand just how fracking affects the environment if secrecy is maintained. Until transparency is achieved, the impacts of fracking will remain nebulous and mysterious and there will continue to be very little anyone can do about it.
