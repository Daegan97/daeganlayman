Scalding Coffee and Exploding Cars

An Argument for Increasing Corporate Risk

Essay on law and philosophy. Written 2018. 7 min read.

Statistics now tell us, often precisely, how likely our actions are to hurt someone. Using the McDonald’s hot-coffee case and the Ford Pinto, this essay argues that the law should hold corporations to what they already know.

The American legal system often uses the notion of foreseeability to determine the proximate cause of harm in cases of negligence. If you could reasonably have anticipated that your actions would cause harm, and that harm did in fact occur, the law can find you culpable for it. As society becomes more statistical, our ability to anticipate harm grows, and with it the range of things we can be held liable for.

Put in enumerated form, the argument runs like this:

  1. If injuries or damages result from an action, and the actor could reasonably have foreseen them, the actor ought to be held accountable for those consequences.
  2. Statistical analysis of human behavior expands the boundaries of reasonable foreseeability by telling us how likely the consequences of our actions are.
  3. Society endorses this quantitative worldview, which legitimizes the expanded foreseeability that social statistics make possible.
  4. Therefore, those who act without regard to the statistically obvious chance that their actions will cause injury should be held legally liable for the consequences.

I will argue that a heavier reliance on statistics in the courtroom can increase the responsibility of actors and serve as a determinant of liability, as the case of Liebeck v. McDonald's Restaurants demonstrates. I will then use the Ford Pinto scandal and deterrence theory to argue that society should raise the risk of corporate crime, which is so often justified by a perversion of the very same statistical reasoning.

Why foreseeability?

The first premise simply restates foreseeability as a basis for proximate cause, so it is worth asking why anyone should accept that basis. The other major type of causation in law is cause-in-fact, or the "but-for" condition: an action causes an outcome if and only if the outcome would not have happened without it. But-for causation works in many cases, but it is easy to imagine where it fails.

Suppose two people, unaware of each other, toss lit cigarettes into dry grass in front of a house at the same moment, and the resulting fire burns the house down. Either cigarette alone would have started the fire. Because both did, neither is a but-for cause, and under that logic neither person is liable. Proximate cause, and with it foreseeability, lets the law assign blame where but-for causation cannot. It is especially useful when the chain of events leading to a harm is tangled, with several actors who may be partly or wholly responsible.

Statistics as the measure of the reasonable

The second premise holds that statistics tell us the risks attached to our actions and so expand what counts as reasonably foreseeable. Applied without limits, foreseeability is nearly infinite. Choosing between two jobs, I can imagine countless futures that follow from each. Holding someone accountable for every imaginable outcome would be absurd, which is why the law limits itself to reasonable foreseeability. But "reasonable" is vague, perhaps deliberately, leaving room for judges and juries to deliberate.

Statistics can reduce that ambiguity. If there is statistical evidence of a harmful outcome and the actor willfully ignores it, the actor should be held liable. In Liebeck v. McDonald's, the company could not justify ignoring hundreds of prior cases of injuries like the plaintiff's from its hot drinks. Its failure to account for that statistical likelihood of harm is what convinced the court that it was liable for Stella Liebeck's injuries. Where other evidence of causation is thin, statistical analysis can do real work.

A society that runs on probability

The third premise may seem contentious until you consider how deeply statistics shape modern life. As the philosopher Ian Hacking puts it, "probability and statistics crowd upon us. The statistics of our pleasures and our vices are relentlessly tabulated… Our public fears are endlessly debated in terms of probabilities" (Hacking 4). For Hacking, statistics have changed how we understand the world logically, metaphysically, epistemologically and ethically. Probability now "lies at the basis of all reasonable choice made by officials… by covering opinion with a veneer of objectivity, we replace judgement by computation" (Hacking 4).

If society and its laws already treat statistical reasoning as legitimate, then the expanded foreseeability it creates is legitimate too, and blame established through statistical foreseeability is a valid basis for finding negligence.

The objection: does this erode personal responsibility?

A common objection is that as human behavior becomes more predictable, personal responsibility shrinks. Suppose that is true. Modern society is thoroughly statistical, and yet people are still held personally responsible for their actions; a glance at America's overcrowded prisons makes that obvious. So even if statistics diminish personal responsibility, they do not eliminate it.

Whether that diminishment harms society is too broad a question to settle here. But in liability cases, trust in statistics lets people injured through negligence force an individual or corporation to change its practices, so that others are not harmed by the same negligence. After Liebeck, McDonald's lowered the temperature of its coffee, sharply reducing the chance that anyone else would suffer burns like hers or like those of the hundreds who came before her. Statistics can be used to reduce suffering, a nearly universal moral value, and so they ought to be used more readily.

The coffee case

Liebeck v. McDonald's Restaurants is a landmark product liability case. Stella Liebeck, an elderly woman, was severely burned by coffee bought at McDonald's. After the company refused to cover her medical expenses, she sued, "alleging that the coffee she purchased was defective because of its excessive heat and because of inadequate warnings" (Cain 15). McDonald's argued that accidents like hers were rare, but records showed more than seven hundred people had suffered similar injuries from its hot drinks.

The verdict in Liebeck's favor fits my argument. When harm is statistically shown to follow from an action, and the actor knew it, the actor should be held accountable. McDonald's knew its coffee temperatures could, and did, cause serious burns. The harm was clear and foreseeable, and the company was capable of preventing it. That it was made to pay Liebeck's medical bills and damages, and was made an example of, was reasonable, not frivolous. The case's popular reputation as the archetypal frivolous lawsuit is simply wrong. And as society becomes more driven by the probabilities of behavior, the degree of foreseeability courts expect of individuals and corporations will keep rising.

Extending the boundaries of reasonable foreseeability may increase the courts' workload, but that work will not be frivolous. Statistical analysis gives courts a way to identify causation where other methods fall short, and it holds corporate activity to a higher standard by widening the scope within which corporations can be found negligent.

The calculus of corporate crime

Contemporary criminal justice treats direct, violent crime as the most heinous, yet Edwin Sutherland "argued that corporate crimes… produced far more social harm than did street crime" (Griffin and Spillane). From a rational-choice perspective, corporate executives make unethical or illegal decisions through a hedonistic calculus that prizes economic gain over social and collateral damage. The same tools of chance and variance that can help courts hold corporations liable are the tools corporate actors use to rationalize harm.

Rational choice theory, drawn from the writings of Beccaria and Bentham, "assumes that people are rational, that their behavior is a product of free will, and that they are hedonistic" (Brown, Esbensen, and Geis). An unethical corporate decision, on this view, is one that has been rationally weighed against the alternatives, made freely, and expected to produce a "good" outcome for the decision-maker, which in corporate life is inseparable from profit. Sutherland went further, suggesting that "the impulse toward corporate profit essentially constitute[s] a criminogenic force" (Griffin and Spillane).

The Ford Pinto is the textbook case. Ford's officials could improve the safety of their early-1970s models at a nontrivial cost or put the car on the market as it was. Using a National Highway Traffic Safety Administration model, Ford estimated "that society loses $200,725 every time a person is killed in an auto accident" (Shaw and Barry). Comparing that figure with the cost of improving 12.5 million vehicles, it concluded that fixing the cars would cost more than the deaths and injuries. Several courts later awarded large punitive damages to people harmed by the Pinto, yet legal technicalities and loopholes let Ford avoid much of that cost despite its culpability.

The same calculus drives other common corporate harms, such as the exploitation of workers "through otherwise legitimate business practices that disadvantageously affect vulnerable workers in product and labour supply chains" (Davies and Ollus). Child labor did not end because its dangers were proven; the evidence was there long before. It ended because hard law made it costlier than it was profitable.

Changing the equation

Deterrence, a core idea in rational choice theory, holds that the threat of harsh consequences discourages a behavior. Without adequate consequences for corporate decisions like Ford's, there is no incentive to change: unsafe products will keep reaching the market, labor will keep being exploited, and corporations will keep acting criminally as long as their actions are never treated as crimes.

If corporate negligence is to be fought, courts and regulators have to acknowledge the calculus executives use and change its variables. Under deterrence theory, greatly increasing the risk of corporate crime would reduce how often it happens. Put simply, the law must make sure the ends, usually profit, can no longer justify the means.

The coffee and the car point the same way. Corporations already live by statistics; they know, often precisely, how many people their choices will hurt. The law should hold them to what they know.
