Symbols of Terror and Simulacra of War

Essay on media criticism. Graduate paper, M.S. Criminal Justice (CSU Global), 2021. 6 min read.

How news coverage turns “terrorist” into an ethnic label, read through social-psychology research and Jean Baudrillard’s essays on the Gulf War. It ends with what audiences, not just outlets, would have to give up.

This essay will examine the effects of media coverage on acts of terrorism and the societal implications of those effects. Of primary concern is the degree to which the label ‘terrorist’ has become a suggestive ethnic epithet in western society and media as well as how terrorism, as symbolic violence, is proliferated when media coverage of it necessarily replicates those symbols. Finally, the question of how consumers of these media depictions of terrorism, through their fixation with fingering the pulse of the real, inadvertently catalyze the depictions of terror, war, and those acts themselves, as the aphrodisiac effect of the symbolic real wanes in effect the more it is consumed.

While “much of the research on media coverage of terrorism has focused on what is covered [and] how it is covered”, the most important element of that coverage “is the meaning of the messages conveyed” (Picard, 1989, 11). In a vacuum, the invocation of terrorism may bring about an idea of abstract political violence intended to achieve some social effect. However, societies do not exist in vacuums. Because of real-world terrorist events like 9/11 and the Charlie Hebdo shooting in France, media and social images of terrorism are constituted by vivid examples that many color their stereotypes of who a terrorist might be with.

Psychologists concerned with the idea that there might be a stereotyping effect with the ‘terrorist’ label constructed an experiment wherein “Sixty White, non-Muslim, British people… were randomly assigned to read either (1) a newspaper article about a terrorist attack perpetrated by a Muslim…. Or (2) to read an almost identical article about a terrorist attack perpetrated by a White non-Muslim” (West and Lloyd, 2017, 213). They asked the participants whether they considered the actions they read about as terroristic or not. The researchers found that “(Muslim) perpetrator ethnicity increased perceptions of the behaviors as terrorism (b = 1.03, p < .001), which in turn predicted negative judgements of the behavior (b = .39, p < .001)” (West and Lloyd, 2017, 215). When presented with explicitly terroristic acts, those participants were significantly more likely to label Muslims as terrorists than their white counterparts. The researchers advocate that “awareness of this bias should encourage a certain amount of caution when using or avoiding the label ‘terrorism’” (West and Lloyd, 2017, 220). Interestingly, although unsurprisingly, the same researchers employed a similar research design to investigate racial prejudice in social perceptions of criminal activity with structurally similar findings.

Despite research suggesting the bias implicit in the invocation of terrorism, it is the case that major news outlets directly utilize bias and political slant to frame the delivery of information to the public. This “absence of neutrality in the majority of media reports, cited by critics across the political spectrum, suggests that these indirect forms of exposure are plagued with bias, influencing the attitudes and perceptions of the public with incomplete coverage of events” (Nickerson, 2019, 549). These distortions of bias transform the information of the real into an unreal caricature. The second-order goal of the unreal is still to deliver information but the first-order goal is to construct a subjective shared perception of that information. In fact, “the news is responsible for providing the ‘pseudo environment’ upon which we rely to experience and understand events we cannot observe directly” (Nickerson, 2019, 549). This pseudo environment serves as the basis for the unreal and thus facilitates the propagation of aggressively violent stereotyping. This framing as it relates to terrorism has been academically realized with the findings of several research studies which “have found that the news media represents Islam as a monolithic, homogenized, or sexist religion, framing Muslims as heartless, brutal, uncivilized, religious fanatics” (Nickerson, 2019, 551). Nickerson posits that “this framing of Muslims and the linkage with terrorism has defined and directed criminality through the activation of racial dehumanization motivations”, essentially providing a socially accepted foundation for anti-Muslim vitriol. Symbiotically, the political machinations of media outlets act in concert with the desire for the public to have an easily perceivable Other to condemn for the violence they are exposed (or expose themselves?) to. How then can society untangle itself from this violent fetishization of the Other under the perceived threat of terrorism?

French philosopher Jean Baudrillard published a series of papers before and during the six-month period of 1991 known as the Gulf War that were and remain remarkably prescient. He proposed that the hostage had taken center-stage over the soldier on the contemporary battlefield. Furthermore, he believed that obsession “governs all our behavior: obsession with every real, with every real event, with every real violence, with every pleasure which is too real” (Baudrillard, January 1991, 28). It is this obsession that fuels the media markets seeking to deliver their interpretations of the real, fragments and half-truths that in turn construct and demonize the Other. Baudrillard laments that “we prefer the exile of the virtual, of which television is the universal mirror, to the catastrophe of the real” (Baudrillard, January 1991, 28). In reality, Baudrillard contended that no one ‘knew’ what was happening in the Gulf War. Those closest to that reality were the civilians and militants who suffered and perished from American aggression. For everyone else, the images and detailing of the war functioned not as lenses into reality but rather symbols of an unreal conflict. Those symbols, reflecting private motivations rather than reality, became simulacra of the war, or copies without originals, with which the “media promote the war, the war promotes the media, and advertising competes with the war” (Baudrillard, February 1991, 31).

By virtue of media provision of wartime simulacra, Americans during the Gulf War were largely captive to a shared perception that justified the war and demonized the Arabic Other in the process. Baudrillard indicted the American people with a charge of a lack of compassion in his second essay, stating “they cannot imagine the Other, nor therefore personally make war upon it. What they make war upon is the alterity of the other, and what they want is to reduce that alterity, to convert it or failing that to annihilate it if it proves irreducible” (Baudrillard, February 1991, 37). Remarkably, this description is historically founded in America’s conduct with Native Americans, black people (intra- and post-slavery), as well as most immigrant groups like the Irish.

To reduce the bane of harmful perceptions and simulacra, it would not be sufficient for the media outlets alone to radically change their behavior, although that is a necessary component of progress. To sufficiently move past the radicalization of the public body against the Other, be that Muslims in the case of terrorism or another Other in a different case, the collective social Self must learn to abstain from the drug-fixation of information and knowledge. Even though “we have neither need of nor the taste for real drama or real war”, societal desire for easily perceivable and categorizable symbols creates a vacuum for media outlets to fill (Baudrillard, March 1991, 75). This is how “the media mix has become the prerequisite to any orgasmic event. We need it precisely because the event escapes us… we have a pressing need of simulation… the obscene aphrodisiac function fulfilled by the decoy of the event, by the decoy of war. Drug-function” (Baudrillard, March 1991, 75).

These words were poignant during the Gulf War and continue to remain applicable to this day when considering the deleterious effect of media portrayals of serious events. Critical examination of ideas news outlets and social pressure pushed on oneself coupled with a conscious denial of the innate desire for any and every depiction of the real would provide social good that, when coupled with more responsible media depictions of the real, would do well to demolish the venom of media influence on society.
