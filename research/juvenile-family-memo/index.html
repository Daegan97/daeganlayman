Memorandum on Policies Concerning Family Factors and Juvenile Offending

Daegan Layman
Graduate paper, M.S. Criminal Justice (CSU Global), 2021. 5 min read.

Abstract. A policy memo arguing, from longitudinal research and an evaluated family-violence program, that the most effective place to reduce juvenile offending is the family, and recommending court-mandated family support programs.

Summary

This memorandum will provide scientific evidence in support of policies that concern the health and quality of parental and family dynamics in relation to tendencies for juvenile offending. Results of studies and programs in the following sections strongly suggest that policies designed to remedy or otherwise alleviate socially negative attitudes, behaviors, and environments on the family- and community-level will produce deterrence and desistence for juvenile offending.

Evidence of the Interrelation between Family Factors and Juvenile Delinquency

Juvenile crime is a national phenomenon that requires unique criminological inquiries to adequately understand and combat its persistence. It is the case that “between 10 and 18% of youth under the age of 18 have been arrested nationwide… yet, decades of evidence suggest that only some youth continue to persist in criminal behavior with age, while most display desistence trajectories” (Cavanagh and Cauffman, 2017, 1533). One of the most pertinent questions concerning this information is: Why?

Before surveying contemporary studies concerning juvenile offending, it is important to acknowledge the recent trend in the last few decades away from “tough on crime” policies in favor of variants of rehabilitative and restorative justice efforts, especially for nonviolent crimes. This trend is felt nowhere more greatly than the realm of juvenile criminal justice. Consideration of the following studies and subsequent policy recommendations hinges on embracing through use of evidence and policy the increasingly apparent societal desire to promote efforts of treatment rather than punishment for most instances of juvenile delinquency. Regardless of concerns about adult sentencing and punishment, juvenile offenders represent a special subgroup of criminal actors that can blossom into continuous offenders even into adulthood or desist and live a law-abiding life. The following studies show support for the general thesis that quality of family and community relationships plays an important role in reducing or increasing the potential for first-time and continued offending.

The last few “decades of research links warm, supportive parenting to a wide range of positive adjustment outcomes for youth… while youth with a negative relationship with their parents may engage in antisocial and health-risk behaviors” (Cavanagh and Cauffman, 2017, 1534). Using a longitudinal design, researchers Cavanagh and Cauffman recorded through interview and self-reporting the relationship between mothers of juvenile offenders before and after any juvenile arrest(s). They found that “mother’s and son’s initial… perceptions of high relationship warmth were associated with sons’ membership in the low-desisting offending trajectory group”, a grouping the study states “does not engage in delinquency” (Cavanagh and Cauffman, 2017, 1541). Those reporting less initial warmth in their relationships were more likely to fall into groupings of serious repeat offenders or offenders variably resistant to deterrence and desistence efforts.

Likewise, according to the research of Wilkinson et al. on National Longitudinal Study of Adolescent to Adult Health data, “a high-quality maternal relationship appeared to moderate the intercept of predicted non-violent offending frequency” and for violent offenders, “quality maternal relationships appeared to moderate both the intercept and slope of predicted violent offending frequency” (Wilkinson et al., 2019, 5-7). This once again provides evidence for the importance of quality parental relationships with their children on deterring juvenile offending. Inversely, juveniles subjected to an inhospitable family environment are more at risk of offending. Wilkinson et al. posit that “maltreatment and abuse are experiences that impact a child or adolescents’ ability to cope effectively with stress which can, in turn, lead to acting out” (Wilkinson et al., 2019, 10). These instances of acting out can manifest as prosecutable criminal behavior and thus, this research provides an insight into one of the vital criminogenic elements for juvenile offending. If it is the case that abusive or otherwise dysfunctional family environments contribute positively to predisposition or continuance of juvenile offending and that positive family environments contribute negatively to juvenile offending, then targeting the family in research and policy is an apt way to target one of the primary factors for juvenile offending.

Finally, there are still elements of family functioning as it relates to potential juvenile offending that require further research. In a study submitted to the Journal of Youth and Adolescence, researchers utilized a structural model based on caregiving and family functioning. They found that “when family functioning is better, youth may perceive less parental monitoring because open communication is more natural within the family” (Folk et al., 2020, 247). In contrast, children subject to dysfunctional family functioning tended to feel a heightened sense of monitoring. This is problematic when considering that “court-involved youth are likely in greater need of parental monitoring than non-court involved youth” (Folk et al., 2020, 247). Children resistant to the perception of heightened monitoring in the family setting will likewise exhibit resistance to court-mandated or court-involved monitoring efforts. This provides a challenge to the implementation and efficacy of policies recommending family intervention for juvenile offenders.

Regardless, there is mounting evidence to suggest that such policies would be correct in their aim to cultivate and sustain beneficial family dynamics for juvenile offenders. Their resistance to those efforts may present a roadblock but that alone should not condemn the effort when the evidence of the good a positive parental relationship can have on juveniles is so readily available.

Policy Recommendations based on Existing Programs for Juvenile-Family Intervention

Based on the scientific evidence provided, it is fair to concur with researchers Kurlychek and Gagnon’s assertion that “adolescent decision-making is… often more easily influenced by peers and the outside environment than that of a more mature adult. Thus, context and peer groups become important in the understanding of adolescent offending” (Kurlychek and Gagnon, 2020, 886). To adequately address the problem of juvenile offending in the community, it is necessary to adopt policies that reflect the importance of family environments for children.

The best way to progress from this acknowledgement is to assess the efficacy of existing treatment models to inform the logistical and programmatic implications of a policy concerning family factors and juvenile offending. For instance, “Step Up is a 21-week parent and youth group intervention for families for which a youth is being consistently violent… in the home” (Gilman and Walker, 2020, 96). Researchers Gilman and Walker found that “participation in Step Up is related to a reduction in future court referrals overall, but not specifically for assault- or [domestic violence]-related offenses” (Gilman and Walker, 2020, 103). Despite this, the evidence compiled gives rise to the notion that “an intervention… can significantly reduce recidivism for youth involved in family violence can reduce the strain on juvenile courts… and decrease… unintended negative consequences” for the families and their youth (Gilman and Walker, 2020, 104).

The best way forward from a policy perspective for addressing juvenile offending is to target the family environment by providing support programs for families experiencing problems with their children and court-mandate those programs for families of juvenile offenders. While the increased monitoring and decreased efficacy for violent juvenile offenders present a formidable challenge to proper implementation and success of such a policy, it would nonetheless provide advantages to the tackling of the problem other juvenile offending policies less concerned with important criminogenic factors which contribute to that offending.

These programs might include parenting education, bonding exercises of community service for the family, and even cognitive-behavioral group therapy. In doing so, children inclined toward offending may successfully be deterred through a boost to their socialization and family function while those who have priors may desist for those same reasons. In all, considering family factors when composing policies targeting juvenile offending is the logical and evidentially substantiated best approach.
