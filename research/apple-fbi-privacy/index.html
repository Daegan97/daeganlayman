An Attack of Terror, An Attack on Privacy

Ethical and Legal Considerations Concerning the Apple Inc.–FBI Feud of 2016

Daegan Layman
Graduate paper, M.S. Criminal Justice (CSU Global), 2021. 8 min read.

Abstract. A defense of Apple’s refusal to build the FBI a backdoor into the San Bernardino shooter’s iPhone, drawing on the DOJ Inspector General’s report, Associated Press v. FBI and the law of corporate privacy.

Like the fervor in 2013 following Edward Snowden’s leaking of classified Central Intelligence Agency (CIA) surveillance documentation, public debate concerning privacy rights flared up once again amidst the feud between Apple Inc. and the Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI) following the acquisition of a locked cellphone related to the San Bernardino terror attack in December 2015. This essay will lay bare the details of that feud, providing a defense of Apple Inc.’s decision not to comply with the FBI’s request to build a backdoor decryption tool for accessing their products. This decision is consistent with the fundamental and constitutionally ascribed rights to privacy persons in the United States have. The notion of privacy itself then will need to be scrutinized as it relates to persons and those constitutional protections which defend that personhood. This will provide an ethically and legally sound conception of a person’s rights to privacy such that the idea of acquiescence with the FBI’s request on Apple Inc.’s behalf ought to be viewed as a violation of both the general rights to privacy as well as the rights ascribed to the specific essence of persons constituting the corporate entity, Apple Inc. and its customer base.

The Facts

Following the injury and deaths of dozens during a domestic terror incident, the FBI, its director, James Comey, and the public at large through the consumption of media coverage were enraptured by the details of the case and their relation to any supposed acts of domestic terrorism yet to occur. In the process of investigation, the FBI “seized pursuant to a search warrant the iPhone of one of the subjects believed to have been responsible for the attack, Syed Rizwan Farook” (“A Special Inquiry…”, 1, 2018). Finding the phone locked, the FBI filed a warrant requesting of Apple Inc. the information on the iPhone. In a 2016 interview, Apple Inc. CEO Tim Cook states while the company complied with the warrant, the government was frustrated by Apple Inc.’s reported inability to unlock the phone (Gibbs and Grossman, 2016). After nearly two months without any further communications with the government, the company was made aware of a suit filed against them by the FBI, asking them to “do the thing we’re [ref. Apple Inc.] calling government OS”: A decryption program which could negate the need for the personal password to generate the decryption key on devices updated to iOS 9 or later typically required (Gibbs and Grossman, 2016). Cook and several others have dubbed a program like this a ‘skeleton key’. Apple Inc. publicly refused on the grounds that such a program would be deleterious to standards of privacy and that it would provide a slippery slope for what the government could compel an organizational entity into revealing about confidential personal data. The FBI continued their attempt to have Apple Inc. comply before withdrawing their legal case, having paid for and utilized a private third-party vendor’s program to crack the decryption.

Interestingly, the Office of the Inspector General in 2018 found misconduct and miscommunication plagued the FBI teams, referred to in the document as OTD, ROU, and CEAU, responsible for attempting to find their own solutions before legally ordering Apple to provide the decryption software, as legally required by an exhaustibility standard. Despite finding “no evidence that OTD had the capability to exploit the Farook iPhone at the time of… Comey’s Congressional testimony” yet certain “FBI statements… were not borne out of the facts” when concerning whether they had indeed exhausted every available option (“A Special Inquiry…”, 9, 2018). Director Comey had claimed the third-party vendor the FBI found and used during the case “had developed the technique by March 16, without any request to prioritize the matter or communication from the ROU Chief, [which was] not consistent with the testimony of the ROU Chief who… had requested the vendor’s assistance a month before” (“A Special Inquiry…”, 10, 2018). Despite the bureaucratic headache, the FBI was successful in decrypting the phone without Apple Inc.’s help. However, the ethical question remains of whether Apple Inc. should have acquiesced to the FBI’s request for a ‘skeleton key’ or if they were morally correct in stating the compulsion to design such a program infringes on theirs and the public’s rights to privacy.

Before that discussion, it is important to address the case of Associated Press v. FBI as it pertains to these events. In 2017, the news organization Associated Press filed a civil action against the FBI, requesting “the identity of the vendor, and the price paid to the vendor” (Chutkan, 1, 2017) under the Freedom of Information Act (FOIA). While the nontrivial price was eventually disclosed, the identity remained exempt under several standards. For instance, in the memorandum opinion for Associated Press v. FBI (2017), Judge Chutkan ruled in the FBI’s favor concerning the confidentiality of the vendor’s identity under Exemption 7(E), agreeing that “releasing the vendor’s identity would risk disclosure of a law enforcement technique and create a reasonably expected risk of circumvention of the law” (Chutkan, 20, 2017). In summary, the FBI was unsuccessful in compelling Apple Inc. to provide a program capable of circumventing their security standards to access a locked phone related to the San Bernardino shooting, but ultimately successful in procuring a solution from a FOIA-immune third-party vendor to grant themselves access.

An Attempt at Defining Privacy

If the outcome of these events seems to the reader troubling or ethically murky, the following definitions and their implications might clarify why exactly that is. Traditionally, one’s rights to privacy can be found in the Fourth Amendment and its “prohibition against unreasonable seizures, have been supplemented by contemporary constitutional rights protecting personal decisions… [and] some kinds of information” (Orts and Sepinwall, 2277, 2015). Privacy is understood then as a blanket term, “connected to freedoms of political speech, religion, and… to protect individual personal dignity and autonomy regarding decisions about sexual behavior and reproduction” (Orts and Sepinwall, 2281, 2015). Despite the difficulty in distilling its essence, there are commonalities between the various invocations of privacy. Generally, rights to privacy are meant to protect the consciously non-public aspects of oneself from being forcibly rendered public. These are rights which are constitutionally granted and extended because the legal armor they provide defends against harms one incurs when otherwise forced to relinquish their private self.

However, a non-public aspect of oneself and a personal aspect of oneself are not necessarily coincident. Just as public and personal information may coincide such as in a person’s social media feed, non-public and non-personal information may coincide. It is without dispute that “a corporation itself cannot hold a ‘purely personal’ privacy right. Just as a corporation cannot be put in jail, it cannot get pregnant” (Orts and Sepinwall, 2287, 2015). How then can a corporation like Apple Inc. refuse to comply with an FBI order on the grounds of such compliance violating privacy? Orts and Sepinwall grant their readers legal scholar Elizabeth Pollman’s eloquent statement that “corporations do not receive rights because the characteristics of the entity so closely resemble a… human so as to merit… the right; rather corporations receive rights because… they have natural persons involved in them” (Orts and Sepinwall, 2287, 2015). By refusing the FBI’s request, Apple Inc. was invoking the rights to privacy held by their agents, stakeholders, customers, and the public body itself. The first three parties follow naturally from the ‘essence of persons’ conception of the corporate entity while the privacy rights of the public body were invoked on the grounds that acquiescence with the FBI might further allow governmental bodies in the United States and the world to compel Apple Inc. and any other technological company rooted somehow in data to reveal private information. Apple Inc. can show their standing by virtue of their corporate personhood and their “right to speak in order to protect citizens’ right to listen”, a fact made apparent in the public arena of the Apple Inc.-FBI feud (Orts and Sepinwall, 2290, 2015).

Ethical Ramifications of The Privacy Debacle

The sequence of events as they occurred has been detailed, as well as how rights of privacy extending from the Fourth Amendment protect individual and corporate persons from the publication of their non-public selves. What exactly then are the consequences the advocates and opponents of this outcome might suggest will occur?

If Apple Inc. had been forced to comply with the FBI request, security experts involved in the amici filing of a brief alongside Apple Inc. in the relevant court proceedings presume that “the FBI might demand next that Apple assist law enforcement by surreptitiously turning on an iPhone microphone or camera…” and generally sense that “the biggest consequence from forced code signing like that ordered in this case could be a general erosion of public trust in software updates” (Brief for Apple Inc., 17-18, 2016). Their myriad list of harms described in depth by interested technological professionals essentially boils down to the notion that granting the court’s request would threaten data security, privacy, and present an unprecedented shift in what the courts can compel a company to do.

On the other hand, legal scholars like Dunlap Jr. worry that “private sectors are getting the ability to say to the government, and to the courts: ‘No, I’m going to determine what the government is going to be able to see or not’” (Dunlap Jr., 1698, 2017). He makes the argument that it would be absurd for a bank to refuse emptying the contents of a safety deposit box upon provision of a warrant and thus likewise it is absurd for Apple to refuse to comply in this case. However, what Dunlap Jr. fails to consider is the novelty of the case in question. Apple Inc. was not asked to provide their key for a digital deposit box. Continuing the metaphor, no such key could be provided because Farook was the only person to have been in possession of those digits and he was deceased. Rather, they were asked to provide a mechanism which inherently eschewed the need for keys in the first place. It may seem trivial, but legal scenarios like these are by-and-large considered and decided on a case-by-case basis so these discrepancies serve as important details under the scrutiny of legal consideration.

Regardless of disagreement, Dunlap Jr. is right to quote journalist Chris Smith when suggesting that “Apple’s intransigence may have backfired… the success of the FBI’s contractor ‘proves what we all suspected: that independent security companies and hackers know to bypass the safety of the iPhone and other devices if need be”, concluding that “in essence, Apple created a legitimate market for hacking its phones on behalf of law enforcement” (Dunlap Jr., 1699-1700, 2017). Despite Apple Inc.’s attempts to resist, the security in place to protect against infringements on digital rights to privacy was circumvented by the FBI through an undisclosed third-party vendor. The government’s continually unfettered access to personal or private information was not hampered in the long run by Apple’s dispute with the FBI.

While law enforcement professionals may have legitimate reasons stoking their fear of ‘going dark’ in the digital age, the consequence of giving in to those fears often leads to constitutional infringements of privacy. Instead of debating privacy versus security, politicians and judges ought to consider the multifaceted ways in which the ideas interlink and interrelate to one another before making decisions that ultimately endanger both.
