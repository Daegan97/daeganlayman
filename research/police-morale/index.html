Assessing and Improving Police Morale and Community Safety in the ‘Post-Ferguson’ Era

Daegan Layman
Graduate paper, M.S. Criminal Justice (CSU Global), 2021. 11 min read.

Abstract. Did public criticism after Ferguson drive down police morale? This paper weighs the evidence, finds a weak but real link, and lays out a plan for regulation, accountability and stakeholder buy-in, with measures to tell whether it worked.

Introduction

This essay will examine the effect that public concerns about police behavior and use of force have on both policing in action and efforts to staff agencies. While racial profiling and police brutality or otherwise wanton use of force has been a reality prior to the 2010s, recent police-citizen fatal shootings have catalyzed a reactionary voice in the public calling for change at both individual and systemic levels to avoid further harm to communities. There is a concern that this stigmatizing of the officer function in society damages the morale of practicing police officers, who in turn become demotivated in the fulfillment of their duties, potentially leading to a rise in crime. The following section will assess the veracity of that claim. Finding there to be at least a weak correlative link between a decline in public opinion and a correspondent demotivation of officers, a plan will be presented to implement changes to regulations and the working environment such that both opinion and morale might be improved. Addressed in this section will be how stakeholders, both internal and external, will be swayed into setting this plan in motion, how it will be communicated to them, and how it will be assessed for success after implementation.

Assessing the ‘post-Ferguson Effect’

The ‘post-Ferguson Effect’ is an idea propagated after the 2014 shooting of Michael Brown by officer Darren Wilson that “negative attention of police and accusations of racial profiling and/or excessive force—was discouraging law enforcement officers from their policing duties… subsequently leading to increases in crime” (Morrow et al. 2019, p. 585). Michael Brown’s death was relatively recent, yet there have been many police shootings that have sparked similar public outcry since 2014. Therefore, analyzing the validity of this idea continues to be a salient effort when discussing police officer morale. Hess, Orthmann, and Ladue (2016) detail in their text on law enforcement management that “key considerations in building morale include salary, the quality of supervision, organizational and public support, physical conditions at work, and favoritism” (p. 322). These considerations intersect with many of the concerns ideated in the ‘post-Ferguson Effect’ theory, namely that de-policing efforts might extend beyond demilitarization and into salary and physical work conditions. Likewise, a lack of public support is the primary nexus between the question of negative attention from troublesome use of force and its potential deleterious effect on police morale. For reference, in 2008, “88% of American citizens had a ‘great deal’ of confidence in law enforcement… but has diminished substantially… [to] less than 57%” in recent years (Deuchar, Fallik, and Crichlow, 2019, p. 1043). This decline is threatening to the legitimacy of policing as a disciplinary institution and therefore it does follow that its continuance might be having a real effect on the morale of officers and potential recruits. While the events and resultant coverage of certain police-civilian altercations (especially those resulting in the death of the civilian) have catalyzed the recent decline in confidence and remain the object of this issue, these details are incidental to the broader claim that demotivation of working individuals can occur when their occupation is regarded negatively in the public sphere. What is unique about the situation of the police in relation to this claim is that the fulfillment of their duties is at least partially contingent on their perceived legitimacy by those they interact with. The delegitimizing effect of an erosion in public opinion can be consciously felt by those in the policing occupation in the news they consume, the interactions they have, and the ease or difficulty experienced in the line of duty.

Studies reviewed for this project concluded from their respective samples of agencies and college students considering majoring in Criminal Justice that there was a mild trend towards both a reduction in morale and increased focus on community policing and a “guardian – rather than a warrior – mindset to build trust and legitimacy with the public” following from a continued decline in public support for police due to situations such as the death of Michael Brown (Deuchar, Fallik, and Crichlow, 2019, p. 1054). This evidence substantiates the idea that policing confidence will continue to be an issue in the field of criminal justice until it is resolved. A potential good-faith interpretation of concerns about policing leading to those kinds of use-of-force issues can set the stage for an agreement with the notion that focusing on community policing and policing ‘as public service’ is the correct path forward.

While it seems natural to assume that any given worker of a service or care profession might feel less motivated in their duties if met with vitriol for their role in society, the role of the police officer is inherently unique “from any other care profession. They… suffer a social stigma full of negative connotations as a consequence of their sanctioning function” (Navarro et al., 2020, p. 120). By their very nature, a police officer alienates themselves from at least those in society who hold disdain for the profession. Unfortunately, this alienation is amplified in the wake of events like those of Ferguson, Missouri, as the number of people in society with growing contempt for policing swells in correspondence with the issues of the time. This alienation and resultant demotivation is not just experienced in the mental realm. Research concerning occupational injury among police officers found that those “who were amotivated… were more likely to believe that injury is inevitable, and that it was sometimes necessary to ignore safety regulations” (Chan et al, 2017, p. 349). The manifestation of a lack of motivation into physical ailments is a concern not just for officers, but for leadership and organizational personnel responsible for the wellbeing (and the costs related to) the officers within the department.

It should be noted as an aside that police use of lethal force did not substantially change following the Ferguson shooting nor would it be safe to assume such a change to be present following any other recent notable incidents. Researchers Vandegrift and Connor (2020), conducting analysis for the Journal of Quantitative Criminology, found that “changes in policing following the events of Ferguson had a generally positive but uneven effect”, decreasing a few tenths of a percentage point for non-black victims and increasing by even less of a margin for black victims over the span of 2013 to 2015 (p. 235). Therefore, the incidents alone or the combination of the incident and its coverage are not sufficient means to incite the kind of change that would alter the status quo in any meaningful manner.

The strain put on the social contract between the public apparatus and the various institutions of order and control has been magnified by numerous cases of excessive use of force accompanied by adamant media coverage. With a focus on engaged and effective policing that is restrained without becoming diluted, as idealized through the ‘guardian mindset’, an effective path forward can be carved that might both restore police morale and public support.

Implementing a Plan

From the understanding established in the section prior that a galvanization of public opinion rallied against the legitimacy of policing institutions can negatively impact the morale of practicing officers, there is a sound argument for the development of a proactive solution to be implemented to remedy what is ailing officers and the communities they serve. While a specific enumeration of the gritty details for such a plan is beyond the scope of this examination, an image can nonetheless be painted with broad strokes. For instance, a plan to restore confidence in the occupation of police, inside and outside the force, will necessarily need to grapple with standards for excessive force as well as accountability (or lack thereof) for scenarios regarded as excessive. Implementation of a plan attempting to solve this would require heightened regulatory standards and consequences requiring intense, methodical reasoning for use of force as well as escalation of force. Likewise, investigations into police misconduct would need to be met with less resistance (i.e., the Blue wall of silence) in favor of cooperation with efforts to ensure the justness of officer actions under an agency’s purview. Holistically, such a plan requires a cultural shift at the workplace level wherein the role of officer and the regulations articulating how an officer ought to conduct oneself should be regarded as instrumental to the ultimate goal of policing institutions to act as guardians of the public in their entirety, rather than an exercise in the power vested in one who claims authority from the law. Through implementation of these and similar substantive changes to the structural functionality of policing, the chance for unjustifiable instances of misconduct will lower, allowing space for what would likely be a slow restoration of public confidence in the institution. As a result of this restoration, officers will be less likely to be demotivated by some of the major external factors capable of affecting their performance, health, and morale. While the extent to which changes such as these could be reasonably implemented without great difficulty is a genuine concern, the failure to secure any degree of likeminded change is likely to result in a furtherance of the decline in public opinion, bolstering the issue at the heart of this analysis. Regardless of whether such changes are implemented directly or incrementally, the question remains of how one might convince the various internal and external stakeholders interested or party to the organizational construct of policing.

For internal stakeholders like officers, police leadership, and police unions, one of the most important benefits from adoption of a plan for change is the resolution of this issue of demotivation because of decline in public opinion. Furthermore, for officers, the presence of a finer set of regulations providing greater clarity on the appropriateness of use and escalation of force could help them feel more comfortable in carrying out their duties and less likely to feel as if they could end up in a situation where they would be the next officer catching heat from the conflagration of media and public opinion. For police leadership, the provision of additional regulations would heighten their capability to maintain a particular control and order to those whom they lead. Likewise, both the communities the leaders serve as well as the media and public officials they interact with would benefit from fewer instances of unjustifiable use of force, easing the tension experienced by leaders when grappling with their twin duties to the public and to their own officers. Finally, police unions, in their emphasis for laborer protections, may in fact be the most difficult to convince of the argument for necessary changes. Such changes would inevitably leave greater room for officers to experience authentic liability for their actions, including misconduct. However, one could argue that the de-prioritization of the need to protect union members, including ones who could be considered as clearly in the wrong, could leave room for the unions, both in funds and focus, for better protections of their members from more legitimate transgressions against their rights as laborers. Likewise, the promotion of morale and worker satisfaction inherent in the goal of this plan ought to be objects of interest for the unions as well, considering it is those workers who comprise their membership.

For external stakeholders then, the question of whether police morale might be restored likely matters less to them than any one of the internal stakeholders in favor of consequences of implementation that would lend favorably to their practical self-interest. For instance, because of changes like the ones suggested, citizens would experience and perceive less harm from police to their communities without compromising the provision of safety police could not offer if they were completely done away with. For media, both the changes and the results of those changes would make for coverage and if implemented properly, could even make for good coverage. This can be seen as an incentivization, although it is only through media’s ability to alter public perception that this stakeholder can be viewed as needing incentivization. Media alone as an apparatus has no direct concern with regulatory changes for policing, but “plays at least some role in shaping perceptions and attitudes about crime and justice” (Levan and Stevenson, 2019, p. 99). Lastly, politicians and judges could be convinced that hardline changes such as these, especially if they were to expand well further from the juridical establishment of justified use of force (Tennessee v. Garner (1985), Graham v. Connor (1989), Scott v. Harris (2007), etc.), if the changes were presented as less financially and socially costly than the status quo. Furthermore, proper implementation of the changes, when assessed, ought to reveal no negative effects of the changes on crime and clearance rates for police agencies, which can also be considered powerful information for the convincing of that subgroup of external stakeholders.

To communicate these changes to all relevant stakeholders, a medley of presentational tools must be used. For the public, official publication of notable regulatory changes and codifications of officer conduct combined with televised conferences hosted by police leadership and open to public questions would serve as effective means for communication. Emphasis would be given to how those changes will affect the community and its health as well as the police serving it, through the lens of physical safety, mental health, and accountability. For police leadership, prior to implementation of any provisions of the plan a structured meeting with experts, evidence, and infographic analysis, presented in a format such as PowerPoint, would likewise be effective. Included in this meeting would be the formative steps taken to establish a specific assessment methodology for evaluative purposes further down the line. For acting police officers, the implementation of these changes would be accompanied by trainings, seminars, and active dialogue between other staff and leadership officials, all accompanied by a codification of a set of rules or regulations enshrining the intent of the plan.

To assess the efficacy of this plan to introduce additional regulatory measures for conduct and accountability in an effort to restore public opinion and reduce the declination of officer morale, there are two levels one must consider: One is the meta-level, wherein analysis must be conducted on police depictions in media and in the perception of the public in comparative relation to the depictions and perceptions prior to the implementation of changes. The other is the ground level, where more traditional means of analysis such as before/after data comparison of clearance rates, instances of crime, officer satisfaction, and documented instances of use of force can be used to determine what kind of effect the changes had.

For the changes to be successful, media depiction would become more favorable and so would public opinion. Clearance rates would remain relatively stable, instances of crime would remain the same or decrease, officer satisfaction would increase, and instances of use of force would have a greater chance of being justified rather than unjustified, even under the context of the new regulatory paradigm, after change is enacted.

Conclusion

There is a weakly correlative element between degradation of public opinion in police and the morale of acting police officers, at least as observed since 2014 after the shooting of Michael Brown by an officer in Ferguson, Missouri. To combat this, change must occur such that cases like that of Michael Brown are made less and less frequent if public opinion is to be restored in such a way as to alleviate the strain and mental harm incurred by officers experiencing cognitive dissonance in the rightful fulfillment of their duties and the deleterious actions of choice fellow officers. Without such change, motivation will suffer further and so too will the communities the police claim responsibility for.
