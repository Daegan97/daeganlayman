An Examination of Military Ethics and the Uniform Code of Military Justice

Daegan Layman
Graduate paper, M.S. Criminal Justice (CSU Global), 2021. 17 min read.

Abstract. How United States v. Briggs (2020) exposed gaps in how the UCMJ handles sex crimes, what GAO data show about racial disparities in military investigations and courts-martial, and why military ethics training should pair virtue ethics with the lessons of situationism.

Written in February 2021. Since then Congress has changed how the military prosecutes sexual assault and related offenses, beginning with the National Defense Authorization Act for Fiscal Year 2022, so parts of the legal picture described here have moved on.

Introduction

The United States Armed Forces adhere to slightly differing value systems, typically promoting the practice of virtues like respect, honor, loyalty, and ethics in general. However, it is apparent across the Armed Forces that a common virtue ethical framework is utilized in the construction and education of how to act properly as a military member. Furthermore, the Code of Conduct enshrined in Executive Order No. 10631 (1955), legally informed by the contents of the Uniform Code of Military Justice (UCMJ) Congressionally ratified five years prior, and disseminated to all members of the military throughout training and regular conduct, asserts in Article VI that a military member shall “never forget that [they are] an American, fighting for freedom, responsible for my actions, and dedicated to the principles which made my country free” (Executive Order No. 10631, 266, 1955 [am. 1988]). This paper will examine the bedrock of military ethics as well as the effectiveness of the virtue framework the Armed Forces situate their moral teachings within and the legal backing of the UCMJ in combatting and controlling ethical violations in the military through examination of court cases and statistics concerning judicial and nonjudicial punishments as well as courts-martial. Finally, it will provide accounts of alternative ethical frameworks suggested for the military setting and attempt to synthesize those findings with military and historically philosophical understandings of character virtue.

Before addressing military virtue ethics and the moral veracity of sentencing and punishment articles in the UCMJ, it is important to address the common objection to virtue ethical theories in general that they presuppose good or bad character traits not only exist within a moral agent but are consistently expressed over time. The experiments of the philosophical and psychological field of Situationism like Stanley Milgram’s shock experiment demonstrate in disparate but equally compelling manners that an agent’s moral predilection can in fact be manipulated in specific instances, through use of authority and power dynamics or any range of influencing factors. Rather than utilizing that notion to deny virtue ethics, it may instead be wiser to allow the information to supplant and transform the old theories. Character traits need not be understood as innate, but ideal. In the same way, it is not necessary that they are consistent over time, but rather that the ideal expression of those traits is a consistent one. The theories and experiments of Situationism have indeed exposed that seemingly good character traits are fragile. Instead of abandoning them outright, these traits must instead be honed and nurtured to keep them from collapsing under pressure. From this understanding, one can begin formulating a precise military ethics educational framework that could prove morally and practically superior to the sometimes diluted and inconsistent ethical status quo which may contribute towards the perpetration of criminal misconduct in the military setting.

Section I: Legal Foundations for Ethical Behavior in the Military

This section will situate the conception of law within a positivist framework before assessing military screening procedures and the case of U.S. v. Briggs. It will be established that the branches of the United States Government act symbiotically in designing standards for the military and provide an instance of sex crimes in the military that almost slipped through the cracks but for the intervention of the Supreme Court. This will provide a transition into an analysis of the systemic failures in prosecuting sex crimes and equitably prosecuting crimes in general experienced by the military, exemplifying the need for evolution and transformation in both the law and philosophy concerning military conduct.

First, it is important to understand the legal deterrents for misconduct, unethical or otherwise, in the military setting. Jurisprudential scholar H.L.A. Hart and several others promoted the idea of legal positivism, or that law is distinguishable and separable from morality in the sense that laws exist only as enforceable codifications of social norms or facts. This is to say that while social norms can be morally relevant, they do not necessarily have to be. Proceeding from this positivist perspective, one can see that the entirety of federal government has and continues to concern itself with military conduct. The Executive Branch like in former President Eisenhower’s case when he established the Military Code of Conduct, issues Executive Orders detailing proper military etiquette. Congress passed and continually maintains the UCMJ, which functions as the foundation for all military law. Likewise, Congress is principally responsible for military funding expenditures and declarations of war. Finally, military courts and the Supreme Court consider cases of prosecutable military misconduct, like in the case of United States v. Briggs decided in December of 2020 and discussed near the end of this section.

Military recruitment screening typically consists of drug tests, credit checks, and assessments like the ASVAB and AFQT. There are also parameters for weight, height, age, as well as citizenship standards that are accounted for in the process of enlistment. Enlistees are subjected to a psychological exam, typically the Minnesota Multiphasic Personality Inventory (MMPI). However, it is the case that there have also been times during this nation’s history that assessment procedures were set to the side in some capacity to allow a greater number of recruits like during the massive increases in drafting during both World Wars and the Vietnam War, so they can be flexible. While any individual component of the screening process may help in some way to get ethically sound individuals into the military, the overall process is inadequate in eradicating the potentiality of prosecutable misconduct in the military setting. If one assumes that virtue traits are ideals, it follows that no vetting system would prove adequate in this endeavor. Instead, these procedures should be considered additive to the more integral experience of emphasizing and shaping those ideals into the actionable moral fabric of enlistees. For instance, rather than ‘testing’ for integrity, the system would be improved by evaluating one’s base sense of integrity, comparing it to the ideal, and committing them to an individualized path of betterment. This process, while more involved, would likely prove superior to any of the screening efforts in producing a virtue-minded military body. The ethical side of this discussion will be advanced further in Section Three.

Where the military justice system most errs is in reliably and equitably prosecuting instances of misconduct within their ranks. Section Two will provide an in-depth examination of inadequacies in the military law concerning sex crimes and statistically significant racial disparities in investigations and court-martialing. For now, an examination of the Supreme Court’s decision in U.S. v. Briggs will serve to provide a narrowly achieved success story in observing the moral spirit of the UCMJ. §856 Article 56(b) of the UCMJ provides the sentence minimum of “dismissal or dishonorable discharge” for the defined offenses of “rape… sexual assault… rape of a child… sexual assault of a child… an attempt to commit [those] offense[s]… [or] conspiracy to commit [those] offense[s]” (10 USC Ch. 47). Likewise, §920(a) Article 120 states that rape can be “punished by death” (10 USC Ch. 47). However, the Supreme Court case of Coker v. Georgia in 1977 barred death sentences for rape under civilian law. The respondents in United States v. Briggs had successfully argued in the initial military court proceeding and the Court of Appeals for the Armed Forces (CAAF) that the Coker v. Georgia decision meant “that they could not, in fact, have been sentenced to death, and therefore the UCMJ’s 5-year statute of limitations applies and bars their convictions” (U.S. v. Briggs, 1, 2020). While Justice Gorsuch filed a concurring opinion agreeing with the decision of the majority but claiming the Supreme Court lacked jurisdiction to hear the case, Justice Alito and the rest of the Court unanimously held that the prosecutions were both rightful and timely. The Court read that the UCMJ’s phrase “punishable by death… as something of a term of art, meaning capable of punishment by death under the penalty provisions of the UCMJ” (U.S. v. Briggs, 1, 2020). They stated that “the UCMJ is a uniform code. As such, a natural referent for a statute of limitations provision within the UCMJ is other law in the UCMJ”, concluding that adoption of the understanding taken by the defense would counter the intent of the Code and the typical judicial interpretation of statutes of limitations. The case was sent back to CAAF for proper deliberation on convicting the offenders.

One can observe quite discretely two main points from this case study. First, that the Supreme Court correctly rectified the errors of the preceding military courts in attempting to quash convictions for rape over technicalities and a misunderstanding of the relation of the offenses to relevant law. Second, the Supreme Court’s observation that a uniform code is self-referent implies that such a code ought to be complete in a way that the UCMJ and its inconsistencies like the discretion to provide a death sentence despite §855 Article 55 of the UCMJ’s prohibition of “cruel and unusual punishment” fail to rise to (10 USC Ch. 47). It is from these two observations that the next section follows.

Section II: Evaluating Aspects of the UCMJ and its (Unequal?) Application

This section will examine several criminal justice reports calling for change at a legal or institutional level in the United States military system, examining specific UCMJ articles and statistics concerning racial disparities in military investigation and prosecution efforts. This paves the way for the assertion that there are indeed issues both within the legal framework and with certain aspects of conduct which, while not actively advocating for it, may nonetheless be conducive for the ideation and commencement of commonsense ethical violations and likewise can be (and might currently be) utilized in a frivolously discriminatory manner.

The UCMJ is federal law. It acts as the basis for all military law, detailing procedures for military conduct, conditions for court-martialing, the establishment of a military court system hierarchical in nature like the district-appellate civilian equivalent, and for pre-trial, trial, and sentencing procedures. Likewise, the decision in U.S. v. Briggs enforces the status of the UCMJ as a model, self-contained code. The articles concerning misconduct are principally informed by the desire to deter that behavior, both in the individual and in general, providing a light utilitarian outline to military legal efforts, as is common in most other aspects of American criminal law. Reformation efforts are right then to focus on amending and revising the UCMJ rather than to propose entirely new sets of laws governing military conduct.

To begin, it is useful to consider again the position of the respondents in U.S. v. Briggs and how their assertions relate to existing military law. In a report to the Joint Service Committee on Military Justice, it is asserted that when rape statutes carried the possibility of a death sentence, “many juries refused to convict defendants for any rape other than those involving aggravated assault… Permitting lesser penalties was designed to increase juries’ willingness to convict defendants in sexual assault cases” (“Sex Crimes and the UCMJ…”, 43, 2001). The juries were unable to ethically justify murder, even of a guilty party, despite any positive deterrent effects the harsh punishment might entail. Reforms in civilian law accounted for this moral discrepancy, allowing for less harsh punishments that nonetheless seek to achieve general deterrence and individual incapacitation. This evolution in civilian criminal law has led to incongruities between military and civilian statutes concerning rape and other criminal offenses. Likewise, many aspects of civilian legal reform concerning sexual crimes “argued that the corroboration requirements and cautionary instructions wrongly stereotyped rape victims as inherently less trustworthy than other victims of criminal attack. This distrust of the victim put her credibility on trial rather than the accused” (“Sex Crimes and the UCMJ…”, 45, 2001). Put simply, one of the clearest elements of inadequacy in the UCMJ is its reliance on anachronistic definitions and procedures for acts of sexual violence. This is not to say that the UCMJ has not evolved over time on these issues to more accurately reflect the social sentiment expressed in civilian laws, but that the evolution through sparse amendments is slow and wholly insufficient when compared to the admittedly still flawed yet nonetheless comparatively superior civilian legal processes. Continued amending of relevant parts of the UCMJ like most of the sections in Article 120 and 125, which currently combine dissonant progressive and arcane understandings of rape and ‘carnal knowledge’ in somewhat incoherent fashion, to provide for the future adequate legal and rightful protection against the perpetration of sexual assault as it is understood contemporarily is desperately needed.

Another more general issue is the lack of established sentencing parameters allowing military judges flexibility in the assignment of sentencing. Another report from the Criminal Justice Review Group suggests massively amending Articles 56 and 57 of the UCMJ to allow for parameters in sentencing, stating that “a sentencing system in the military must reflect the unique offenses under the UCMJ, and must serve the dual goals of justice and discipline… [It] must be flexible enough to adjudge any lawful sentence when appropriate” (Effron et al, 512, 2015). Accounting for the gradient nature of the severity of the offense would allow for justice to be more adequately observed in the military justice system, largely via the same logic as that of introducing lesser penalties for the crimes of rape and sexual assault to bolster successful prosecutions in the civilian juridical space. A judge is more likely to deliver a sentence that seems commensurate to the offense and thus feels is a sentence justly delivered. Allowing for the nuance of any given criminal activity or breach of conduct to be scrutinized against parameters rather than minimum sentencing guidelines might achieve the effect of crimes being more ably prosecuted, even if it is at the price of prosecuting those offenders less grievously.

Finally, a pertinent issue at hand in the military justice system is the lack of transparency in investigative and prosecutorial efforts and the possibility that justice is delivered in a racially disparate manner. The Government Accountability Office (GAO) describes substantive issues with data collection pertaining to race and ethnicity in their databases and their investigations in their testimony before the Subcommittee on Military Personnel, Committee on Armed Services, and the House of Representatives. While they do preface their findings by stating that “taken alone, [they] do not establish whether unlawful discrimination has occurred”, the supporting statistics delivered by the GAO may prove useful in the legal determination of the existence or nonexistence of that discrimination (Farrell, 1, 2020). Across the Armed Forces, the GAO found that black servicemembers were typically twice as likely to be subjected to recorded investigations and general/special courts-martial than white servicemembers. According to the GAO’s collected data, black servicemembers were, in the most extreme case of the Marine Corps, 2.07 times as likely as white servicemembers to be subjected to recorded investigations and, in the least extreme case of the Air Force, still 1.51 times as likely as white servicemembers to be subjected to courts-martial. Even in the case of nonjudicial punishments and trials in summary courts-martial, black servicemembers were 1.87 times as likely as white servicemembers to be subjected to trials and 1.77 times as likely to receive nonjudicial punishments. Relevant statistics on Hispanic servicemembers show similar discrepancies, averaging out to around 1.4 times as likely as white servicemembers to be subjected to investigations, trials, and punishments combined (Farrell, 3-25, 2020). The GAO reports that “racial disparities generally were not present in convictions or punishments”, but that observation alone does not preclude the possibility of wrongdoing on the military’s part in unequally pursuing investigations and charges against black and Hispanic servicemembers and thus attempting to deliver justice on racially unequal terms. These statistics suggest that policing for misconduct is racially focused, which is unethical. Legal examination is needed to further this claim; however, the statistics provided above stand alone in implying something deeply troubling about the situation across the Armed Forces’ military justice systems and necessitate further research, analysis, and possibly action.

Section III: In Search of the Optimal Framework for Military Ethics

This section will provide several criteria for the model military ethical system: 1) That awareness of power dynamics and relations are an important prerequisite to a morally involved decision, 2) that duty and responsibility are shared ‘up-the-chain’; therefore, officers or otherwise high-ranking officials are obligated to promote ethical behavior in those who answer to them, 3) that the ideas of Situational moral philosophy can be additive to virtue ethics instead of excluding or denying it. Instead of denying it, an approach wherein situational ethics and virtue ethics are considered in concert is 4) the optimal kind of military ethics. The assertion will then be made that the various branches of the United States military are right in emphasizing a virtue ethical code of behavior but fail to capitalize on it; a consistent, well-informed, and carefully defined universal code of idealized virtue ethics backed by a modernized UCMJ could reasonably put a dent in sexualized, racialized, inhumane, or otherwise unethical military conduct.

Military operations of the United States are necessarily entangled in the States’ international relations and foreign policy stances. Practical and theoretical knowledge concerning those international relations, both in the particular and in general, ought to be maintained and reinforced throughout the entirety of the military’s operators. There are obviously kinds of information that necessitate security clearance and good status as prerequisites for access but continuous education, critical discussion, and a formal recognition of the importance of being informed about the effects of one’s work would invariably improve the military milieu. The democratization of information would likewise provide added legitimacy to military orders and establish a meaningful foundation from which unjust orders might be challenged in a way that is, if not affirmed, at least understood.

This clarification of power dynamics in the military environment has an ethical basis. Eva van Baarle and her fellow researchers discuss in a research article on military ethics Foucault’s idea of the art-of-living and the exploration of power dynamics he wrote about in his books on the ward, the prison, and sex. While Foucault’s lessons of being critical of objects and relations of power do “not provide a clear structure through which one can arrive at a morally responsible decision”, it is nonetheless the case that “acknowledging and discussing power relations and tensions, can be regarded as both a pre-condition for how to deal with moral dilemmas and as part of ethical deliberation and decision-making” (Baarle et al., 140, 2018). Likewise, “military officers have a duty not just to keep informed of potentially controversial policy developments, but also to educate themselves on the relevant legal, moral, and Constitutional doctrine” (Seagren, 190, 2015). Without continuous education in the realms of morality and the law across all servicemembers, the military apparatus fails professionally. Dissemination and discussion of the intent and rationale of orders would bolster camaraderie through shared experience and the idealized expression of moral virtues through critical interrogation of motives across ranks. Combined with the continued evolution of the UCMJ to better reflect the law as society wills it, the military body would be socialized and incentivized towards justice and common morality more than the shallow grouping of individuals deemed fit expressed through screening techniques and inconsistent law alone.

Seagren’s article goes further by analyzing how unethical decisions in the military may not necessarily be personal ones. They posit that duty and responsibility are shared ‘up-the-chain’; therefore, officers and other high-ranking officials have a moral obligation to ensure the ethical conduct of those who answer to them. Likewise, officials cannot absolve themselves of responsibility because of their lack of personal involvement with the issue at hand. They are instead morally obligated to resolve apparent or foreseeable ethical issues. The Foucauldian idea of questioning power can be combined with Seagren’s idea of moral responsibility across agency lines to provide a dialogical and Socratic framework for the establishment and maintenance of military virtue at practice.

It is true that traditionally, “the virtue theoretic approach to ethics locates moral value primarily in the character of the agent rather than in the rules governing an act or the consequences that follow it” (Jensen, 101, 2017). In fact, this conception is by no means deficient on its own. Rather, it is the conception that an agent’s character is steadfast and unmalleable that is erroneous. In advocating for education and a honing of purpose above all else, the arguments presented in this paper hinge on the idea of moral development, as opposed to innate virtue. It does not, however, hinge on a strict denial of the military institution and its practices. Virtue ethical theory is “not concerned with explaining how we might flourish in spite of our institutions, but more optimistically provides a road map for the creation and development of excellent institutions in which we can flourish together” (Jensen, 113, 2017). The progression must be symbiotic. Servicemembers, the United States Government, and the institutions of the Armed Forces themselves, would do well to promote progressive changes to the status quo that benefit one another, through amendments to the UCMJ as well as mass reconceiving of power dynamics and the moral efficacy of orders throughout the military hierarchy.

However, situational absolutism alone cannot adequately provide a proper means of conduct for military servicemembers and “although commanders need not resort to dispensing Cinnabon-like aromas over battlefields to ensure that their soldiers act ethically, they should guard against acting or commanding from the assumption that most of their soldiers… have richly developed virtues” (Cartagena, 166, 2017). The idea of military virtue must be understood as a fragile and constantly developing state of being. Ethicists and policymakers “should not, as many moral philosophers have done who were caught up in the situationist debate, identify themselves with a position that puts all emphasis on either character or the situation, to the exclusion of the other” (Olsthoorn, 88, 2017). It is the intersection of character and circumstance that should reign supreme in the discussion of military ethics and the theoretical implications for military law and conduct. The idea that situational moral philosophy is additive to military virtue ethics, rather than exclusive or superior in some way to it can allow for the substantiation of one and another in a symbiotic fashion. The construction of military ethics as a type of virtue ethics was the correct decision, but its current nature is not satisfactory. Instead, it will be precise virtues, values, and appreciation for the nuance of a situation to stir thought concerning one’s moral conduct that will stand as the ultimate realization of military ethics.

Conclusion

The United States Government and its military apparatus have quite a few issues to iron out to achieve a consistent and ethically sound system and body. Chief among these issues is the need to modernize several sections of the UCMJ, namely those in the Sentencing and Punitive Articles sections, to exist more in-line with the advancements made in correspondent civilian law, and for the expression of that law to be delivered in a consistently equitable manner. Likewise, these changes should be met with an effort to hone and focus the disparate virtue traits the branches of the military emphasize to account for the situational nuances of military operations and what exact ideals the model soldier ought to strive to embody. The government would do right in listening to calls for legal and philosophical modernization, for themselves, for the public, for the international communities they interact with, and especially for those who serve.
