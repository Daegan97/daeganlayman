A Policy Critique of Nevada Administrative Code 228

Domestic Violence Treatment Programs for Offenders and Victims

Daegan Layman
Graduate paper, M.S. Criminal Justice (CSU Global), 2021. 13 min read.

Abstract. Nevada requires domestic-violence offenders to complete treatment but lets minimally trained staff deliver it. This critique argues for higher qualifications, backs the case with recidivism research, and works through funding, staffing and stakeholders.

Introduction

This essay will assess Chapter 228 of the Nevada Administrative Code (rev. 2020), which provides the law concerning how domestic violence treatment programs are certified, staffed, and operated in Nevada (Domestic Violence, NAC 228.010-228.655, 2020). Nevada statute NRS 33.018 (2010) requires those convicted of domestic violence to participate in such a program, which are typically geared toward anger management and (if applicable) substance abuse treatment (Acts which Constitute Domestic Violence, NV Rev Stat § 33.018, 2010). However, according to the National Coalition Against Domestic Violence (NCADV), "48.1% of Nevada women and 30.9% of Nevada men" are subjected to domestic violence, sexual violence, or stalking at some point in their life (NCADV, n.d., 1). Furthermore, the State of Nevada Office of the Attorney General (2019) found that recidivism rates for domestic violence in the state over a period of three years from release was 23%, or almost 1 in 4 offenders recidivating (Speights et al., 2019, 3). The provision of a treatment program in and of itself is great policy but this statistical information informs the opinion that the implementation of such a program is necessary in theory, yet deficient in practice. The following arguments presented will illustrate why the best choice for Nevada’s political body is to revise their administrative codes on domestic violence to include higher educational standards across the board in an effort to produce a chilling effect on recidivism rates.

Section One: Deficiencies in the Educational Standards for Domestic Violence Batterer Intervention Treatment Providers per NAC 228.108(b-e)

Nevada law requires that, at minimum, those convicted for charges related to domestic violence must participate “at least 1.5 hours per week for 6 to 12 months” upon first offense and for 12 more upon a second on top of their community service, fine, and/or imprisonment (Nevada Legislative Counsel Bureau, 2016, 3). This section will examine issues with the provision of that treatment.

The primary issue of Nevada's Administrative Code concerning domestic violence offender treatment which may contribute to a lack of efficacy in terms of recidivism is that the qualifications for relevant positions working within the program: treatment program directors are only required to have completed high school and a year of supervisory experience (Qualifications and Duties..., NAC 228.108(b-e), 2020). Likewise, treatment providers are employed as independent contractors to the state and are only required to have any bachelor’s degree and “at least 15 hours of… training in domestic violence”, supplemented by an additional 45 hours once on the job (Qualifications for Supervisors…, NAC 228.110(7a-d), 2020). That training is given by the supervisor of the program who has qualifications similar to that of providers in other states, like Colorado, possessing a graduate degree “in a field of clinical human services” with years of relevant counseling or supervisory experience (Qualifications for Supervisors…, NAC 228.110(1a-e), 2020). Despite this, their contact with offenders participating in the program is minuscule compared to the providers. According to NAC 228.115 (2020), the supervisors meet monthly with each of their providers and observe a single group counseling session for each pairing of providers every three months (Duties of Supervisors… NAC 228.115(1), 2020). The qualifications and duties for all of these positions within Nevada intervention programs are dissonant and do not adequately address the severity of the problem of domestic violence and its recidivation.

For instance, the nature of the provider’s work is essentially intensive psychological counseling for a subset of violent offenders. Other states have requirements for their treatment providers to reflect that nature, largely mirroring the qualifications of Nevada’s program supervisors, so Nevada following suit would allow them to cease lagging behind comparative interstate standards for intervention program job requirements. A single college credit in Domestic Violence and any bachelor’s degree can allow one to work as a treatment provider in the state of Nevada. Despite the intent of the programs to reform and assimilate nonviolently the prior offender back into society, it is nonetheless the case that these individuals who come into the programs are, according to the state, violent criminal offenders. Despite this reality, a person with no prior counseling experience and only the slightest theoretical grasp on the problem of domestic violence is an individual the administrative code sees fit to deem appointable to the position of interacting and treating these offenders on a routine basis.

While the supervisor of a batterer intervention program in Nevada has respectable qualifications and duties, their educational experience qualifications are necessary across the board for all vital positions of the program. Directors and treatment providers having shallow education in the heavily involved subject matter endemic to the intervention program is likely to obfuscate both the goals and results of the program as well as the policy direction the program might take.

Unfortunately, the problems of intervention programs in Nevada extend beyond educational deficiencies. Lack of attendance “in rural parts of the state, suggest[] offenders’ proximity to the program’s location was key for treatment efficacy” and that as of 2018, online treatment alternatives are being offered to those who live far away from program locations (Speights et al, 2019, 5). Frustrating limitations coupled with a disconnect in treatment with theoretical and practical application of behavioral management and clinical service techniques brought about by a lack of experience or education is bound to increase the tension experienced by the program participant and thus damage the quality and effectiveness of the program on them.

Because the goal of these treatment boards ostensibly is to provide the means for domestic violence offenders to refrain from recidivating, it makes sense to codify and subsequently invest in means to decrease recidivation like better educational standards. The following section will examine how effective intervention therapy can bolster the efforts of the state in reforming prior offenders.

Section Two: The Importance of Intervention Therapy for Reducing Likelihood of Recidivism in Domestic Batterers

This section will demonstrate why batterer intervention programs are necessary, how they are most properly implemented, and examine the potential consequences of ineffectiveness in Nevada’s case for failure to adhere to the standards and rigor of programs found in other states through the use of experimental and statistical analyses conducted on intervention program participants.

The criminal justice and social service systems provide domestic violence programs for victims of domestic violence as a reactive measure against the crime of domestic violence for the purpose of protecting the victim and, when functioning effectively, provide support such that “the lives of survivors and their families can only be enhanced” (Sullivan, 2018, 128). Likewise, batterer intervention programs are required by states for offenders as a reactive measure against the crime of domestic violence for the purpose of protecting the victim and ensuring societal and community safety from any further perpetuation of that or other crimes by the offending individual. Victim restoration programs facilitated and funded by state legislatures in an effort to have goals therein of helping survivors and promoting individual and societal wellbeing that can be cross-applied to batterer intervention programs in such a way as to suggest that the best reactive approach to domestic violence is two-pronged, or that attempts to resolve the problem from both sides of the table, ensuring the safety and wellbeing of the victim while rehabilitating the offender, are the most effective efforts. The situation of the domestic violence victim is grave to the point that psychological therapy and intervention for them and their children is necessary. If the experience of domestic violence is inherently traumatic, then the act of committing domestic violence is inherently dehumanizing and so far-removed from community and social standards of human rights and worth to necessitate psychologically intensive examination and therapy to correct that behavior. This kind of work is suited for counselors, therapists, or other psychological experts, not freshly graduated college students.

In a study comparing recidivism rates of domestic violence offenders who completed and who did not complete treatment programs in Ohio, demographic and historic information was collected on a 202-person sample (Lauch, Hart, and Bresler, 2017, 550). Upon comparison, it was found that “the men who completed the program had a significantly lower rate of criminal offending (n = 14; 6.9% of overall sample) relative to the men who did not complete the program (n = 31; 15.3%), which was statistically significant, χ2 = 8.70, p = .003” and that those who did complete the program were more likely to be employed, overcoming the hurdle of the cost barrier presented by the required payments for program participation (Lauch, Hart, and Bresler, 2017, 551). The program models examined in Lauch and his fellow researchers’ study largely embraced the “Duluth model… a psycho-educational approach to treatment with overall goal of re-socializing batterers”, advancing the claim that properly informed psychological intervention programs are a significant means to combat the potential for domestic violence recidivism. It is vital to note that propensity for recidivation was lessened upon completion of programs that utilized qualified treatment providers applying intensive psychological standards upon participants. In the case of the typical offender, psychological treatment is conducive. However, this success is amplified in cases of domestic violence involving offenders who are drug-dependent.

The importance of behavioral or psychological intervention for cases of domestic violence is amplified in scenarios involving serious drug dependence. Particular sets of cognitive-behavioral therapeutic techniques for substance-dependent domestic violence offenders can reduce not only substance dependence but also reduce likelihood of domestic violence recidivism, according to the work done by researchers writing for the Journal of Marital and Family Therapy. Using an experimental design on 63 participants of a substance abuse clinic, researchers found that treatment utilizing cognitive-behavioral techniques resulted in greater attendance by offenders as well as less drug use and considerably less violent recidivism within a three-month period after completion of the program (Easton, Crane, and Mandel, 2018, 493). This trial was the first of its kind “to demonstrate the effectiveness of an integrated, individual therapy approach for male substance-dependent [intimate partner violence] IPV offenders to reduce subsequent IPV behavior” (Easton, Crane, and Mandel, 2018, 495). While these promising results only explicitly involve substance-dependent domestic violence offenders, its conclusion and advocacy for the application of cognitive-behavioral techniques to offenders is important on a universal level and will be used to promote the idea that proper psychological therapy is instrumental to reducing recidivism in domestic violence offenders.

Beyond a sound psychologically minded foundation, what makes for a model batterer intervention program? Utilizing an interview strategy with participants of and staff for intervention programs, researchers found that most “support the notion that there may be a greater opportunity for sustained behavioral change in longer, more intensive programs” (Morrison et al, 2017, 486). There are several conclusions provided throughout but the most important one, in terms of the project, is that facilitators should have specialized domestic violence training because of the unique nature of their profession (Morrison et al, 2017, 487). While Nevada does this in part, it is only in the most limited of ways and fails to capitalize on the psychological and behavioral investment necessary to properly engage with offenders in a way that maximizes the positive effects of the program on the behavior of those offenders.

Why are these programs so important? Put simply, it is because domestic violence is such a massive problem, both in the United States and on a global scale. In Nevada, it is the case that “one domestic violence offense [occurs] every 17 minutes, 20 seconds” (Ford, 2019, 179). Of the 30,303 domestic violence offenses that occurred in Nevada in 2017, only 42% of offenders were arrested despite the fact that 85% of victims displayed observable injuries (Ford, 2019, 179). In the category of “Strongest Mitigating Circumstance for Not Being Arrested”, 9,700 or nearly a third of the total offenders are listed as having “unknown” reasons for not being arrested (Ford, 2019, 179). These baffling statistics illuminate the fact that domestic violence is 1) a destructively pervasive issue in the state of Nevada and that 2) the fight against it is stifled outside of the realm of treatment and so the failures of treatment programs will only reify the extent of the problem in the state.

Section Three: Barriers to Reform

It has been established that Nevada’s legislature would do well to revise some of their qualification standards for the staff of batterer intervention programs. There are unfortunately a few problems that stand in the way of successful lobbying and implementation of the policy that may in fact be part of the reason why the law continues to stand as it is now.

There are three main potential barriers to reform of Nevada’s Administrative Code 228 as it stands. These include stakeholder concerns regarding 1) increased funding required for supporting more qualified staff, 2) insufficient staffing upon adoption of the revised policy, and 3) losing staff that ‘become’ unqualified for their positions. This section will qualify who the primary stakeholders are in this advocation for reform and then examine each of the listed concerns.

The stakeholders involved in a decision on whether to revise Nevada’s Administrative Code 228 are numerous, including domestic violence victim advocates, judges, police officers, legislators, and domestic violence victims themselves. While their individual opinions may vary on certain things, the collective stakeholder mentality concerning domestic violence surely holds that it is societally and individualistically detrimental and as such should be tackled both preventively and reactively in an effort to reduce it. Domestic violence victim advocates may be resistant to funding put towards the ‘betterment’ of offenders in favor of more punitive measures against them but the chilling effect on recidivism rates experienced when offenders receive proper treatment via a program magnified by better educational standards could provide an acknowledgement of value in the face of initial resistance if this were the case.

Funding could be provided through the use of a penny sales tax or a comparative allocation, as Oklahoma did for their MAPS 4 program which funds several criminal justice centers in the state (https://www.okc.gov/government/maps-4). The issue of lobbying for that funding would be simplified by targeted revisions to the law and specific salary allocations for the newly qualifiable positions. At first, the increased cost may seem like a sunk one but recidivism rates for domestic violence are consistently monitored by research agencies both inside and outside of the state and so it is the case that observable changes in recidivism rates for domestic violence offenders participating in intervention programs may be noticed after a few years under the newly revised guidelines.

The issue of staff becoming unqualified can be rectified in the immediate sense by grandfathering those devoted to achieving the newly codified educational qualifications can still work and the state could seek to hire several qualified candidates prior to ratifying the administrative amendment or at the very least, enforcing it in such a way as to hamper the availability of the program in any form to applicable participants.

The most promising route would be to continue their employment provisionally in some capacity while offering the means for them to achieve the proper education such that they can continue their employment on a long-term scale while adhering to the newly revised policy. Increased funding would be necessary but the widespread nature of domestic violence and its largely bipartisan condemnation as a phenomenon on a national scale would make those funds comparatively easier to lobby for than more controversial program bids.

Whatever manner the staffing issue is handled at the immediate time of policy revision adoption, it is nonetheless the case that as long as the overall trend of the programs is to hire and retain more adequately qualified staff than before within a reasonable period of time after amendment of the code will prove to be more conducive than the status quo. Likewise, that continued staffing development of those programs towards more qualified employees will reach an apex such that Nevada batterer intervention programs are staffed in full accordance with the revised Nevada law and operate comparably to other states’ programs. This would be the optimal realization of these newly revised standards, whose effectiveness could then be compared against years prior to the revision on the grounds of quantitative measurements of recidivism rates and participation rates as well as qualitative examinations of programs from the perspective of both treatment providers and program participants. An extensive policy evaluation of the effectiveness of the administrative code revision would likely be comparable to the studies examined in section two, revealing a trend towards success in terms of lowering recidivism rates and contributing to the fight against the perpetuation of domestic violence in the state of Nevada.

Conclusion

Nevada’s legislature ought to update their policies for domestic violence batterer intervention programs to assist in their fight against the occurrence of domestic violence and the potential for recidivism in prior offenders. There is overwhelming evidence suggesting that properly designed intervention programs are effective at chilling rates of recidivism and can function as a reliable reaction to the crime of domestic violence in relation to deterrence and correction of the offender. Despite barriers to reform, most if not all relevant stakeholders could be rallied into change if it were presented in a targeted and specific manner with clear indication of the intent to measure recidivism rates after adoption of the amendment to the code and compare those rates with that recorded prior for the purpose of policy evaluation. This change to Nevada’s Administrative Code 228 could realistically have a noticeable effect on the problem of domestic violence and offender recidivation in the state of Nevada and is worth legislative consideration.
