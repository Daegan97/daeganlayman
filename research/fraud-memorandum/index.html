Memorandum concerning Potential Fraud at Colorado National Bank

Daegan Layman
Graduate paper, Fraud Examination and Prevention, M.S. Criminal Justice (CSU Global), 2021. 13 min read.

Abstract. An investigative plan written as a memo to a bank’s security director after an anonymous embezzlement tip: predication, a fraud theory, evidence and records, legal options, interview order and the final fraud report.

A course exercise built on a hypothetical fact pattern. It does not describe a real investigation of any real bank or person.
To
CNB Security Director
From
Daegan Layman, Fraud Investigator
Date
September 26, 2021
Subject
Fraud Investigative Plan for the Teller CNB branch

In the sections that follow, an investigative plan will be detailed and recommended for evaluating the veracity of the anonymous letter received by the CNB security department alleging that embezzlement is occurring at the Teller, CO bank location. Despite a lack of specificity on how the fraud might be occurring, there are several control risks as described in the fact pattern which could theoretically allow for fraud to occur and be concealed within the work design and dynamic between tellers and managers. Furthermore, research on a large sample of firms confirms a positive correlation between anonymous tips and fraud detection such that “organisations with ARCs [anonymous reporting channels] detect a higher incidence of fraud and a greater number of frauds” (Johansson and Carey, 2016, p. 406). It is important then to consider the anonymous tips that do come through appropriate channels, especially when they seem both reasonable and particularly harmful if true. The presence of control risks further gives weight to the possibility of the reality painted in the anonymous letter. Therefore, predication is sufficient, although not incredibly strong.

Regardless, where predication exists, there is reason to investigate. Put another way, “fraud investigation takes place when indicators of fraud… suggest that a fraudulent act has occurred and requires investigation” to uncover who the fraudster is and just how much they have stolen (West Virginia University, 2007, p. 7). At first, this means hypothesizing and testing. When there is continued reason to believe fraud is occurring or has occurred, an investigative plan meant to uncover and scrutinize the assets and net worth of relevant parties is developed to assist the overall investigative process. The following sections will first describe a theory and plan developed to address the situation described in the fact pattern before assessing the types of evidence necessary to conduct the investigation as well as how to obtain that evidence. Then, legal routes for CNB to follow in response to the alleged fraud will be considered.

Following these sections, further discussion will be given to how public and private records will be utilized to obtain the aforementioned evidence. A fraud interview strategy will be developed and a plan for how to interview the primary fraud suspect will be developed using the fraud interview process. Finally, the completion of a fraud report to summarize all relevant findings will be recommended. Following all of these steps, the Security team at CNB, with support from fraud examiners and other relevant parties, ought to be able to assess whether fraud is occurring and address it properly.

Developing an Investigative Plan through the Investigative Process

Because predication exists, it is necessary to develop an investigative plan to assess whether fraud is actually occurring. Gottschalk (2019) elegantly states the purpose of a fraud investigation “is to reconstruct the past” (p. 112). Generally speaking, a plan will include a theory supposing what and how the fraud is occurring as well as the identification and procurement of evidence like financial records. If the evidence is strong enough to proceed with the investigation, then the Association of Certified Fraud Examiners (2016) recommends the completion of the investigation through “interviews, document examination [and] observations” until the fraud examiner can accurately reconstruct the events such as to ascertain whether or not fraud occurred (p. 3.111). Finally, a fraud report summarizing the findings and recommending a route forward, like pursuing legal remedies. Within this framework, a fraud examiner must consider the scope of the investigation, to include the timeframe, resources, and overall goals of the investigation (Association of Certified Fraud Examiners, 2016, p. 3.141). Specifically, a fraud theory concerning the situation at the Teller CNB bank branch is presented below (these are not declarations of fact but rather hypotheses to be tested throughout the investigative process):

  1. Some of the employees at the bank branch are embezzling money from CNB and its customers.
  2. To conclude that fraud has occurred, an investigation must uncover the misappropriation of assets or evidence of concealment thereof. Furthermore, evidence collected through public and private records, interviews, and financial documentation will be necessary and only if those pieces of evidence contribute substantially to the allegations will fraud be determined to have occurred.
  3. Although no discrepancies have been reported, it has been a while since audits were conducted. Therefore, it is possible that evidence exists or did exist of fraud but is being concealed by the perpetrators.
  4. Because a) “tellers have the authority to cash checks of up to $5000 without having a supervisor review the transaction” and b) “it is not uncommon for the head teller [to] give a teller the override code if she is busy working her own cash drawer”, there exists the capability for tellers to engage in check fraud. Furthermore, this engagement does not necessarily render the head teller culpable (although arguably incompetent by nature of organizational design allowing for the internal control override of sharing the code).
  5. Because the anonymous tip alleges that those involved are receiving kickbacks, lifestyle changes such as new luxury vehicles or other unexplainable purchases (i.e. purchases funded from what a net worth analysis would label ‘funds from unknown sources’) ought to be regarded as potential evidence of fraud, and certainly indicators of its possibility.
  6. Covert surveillance of the bank’s busier time periods may be beneficial to gaining evidence of the occurrence of fraud.
  7. If evidence collected in the process of examining the allegations is fruitful towards the claim, the investigation ought to proceed to the interviewing and examination stage. Those determined to be least involved or least likely to be involved ought to be interviewed first in a gradual progression towards a final set of interviews with the primary fraud suspect.
  8. Upon completion of these steps, a fraud report will be completed summarizing the findings and recommending further action.

Evidentiary Concerns

Albrecht et al. (2019) maintain that information relevant to a fraud investigation can be collected from federal, state, or local sources, private sources, online investigative tools, and simple web searches (p. 247). If one considers the fraud triangle when examining this case of fraud, it is important as well to seek evidence of pressures on employees, whether quantitatively or qualitatively. Certified Public Accountant David Asselstine (2019) asserts that understanding staff on an individual basis, including the pressures they may be experiencing and that they, like anyone, are capable of “rationaliz[ing] and commit[ting] fraud”, is instrumental to a company’s health against fraud and thus is in the best interest of the Fraud Examiner as well (p. 25).

For this case, the most pertinent evidence to unravel a kickback scheme would be records of communication or conversion indicative of such a scheme. Perhaps most easily, a fraud examiner can check the records the company keeps for all tellers at the bank branch to assess whether financial difficulties or other concerning situations were disclosed to the company upon hire. Likewise, the CNB Security department and/or the CNB IT department ought to be able to give access to employee e-mails. Unfortunately, personal e-mails are likely to require subpoenas to access. Examination of electronic evidence like e-mail records “is a highly technical task that must be performed correctly…”, to include the cloning of the examined device so that any legal proceedings following the investigation cannot claim that evidence tampering has occurred (Albrecht et al, 2019, p. 212-213). Public records like land titles and car registrations can be procured to identify whether any of the suspected tellers have experienced unexplainable lifestyle changes contributive to the hypothesis that fraud is occurring or otherwise has occurred.

Similarly, documentary evidence ought to follow a specific chain of custody and copies should be produced for the fraud examination while originals are kept secure for a potential trial (Albrecht et al, 2019, p. 227). It is possible to request documentation through request or through provision of a search warrant, although these methods and the subpoena duces tecum procurement method might arouse suspicion in any perpetrators who might exist at the CNB bank branch. Finally, checks processed and documentation of daily activity ought to be scrutinized by the fraud examiner for inconsistencies, suspicious markings or signatures, and other oddities.

Legal Concerns

Albrecht et al (2019) detail the three primary choices “most organizations and other fraud victims usually make… (1) pursue no legal action, (2) pursue civil remedies, and/or (3) pursue criminal action” (p. 81). Depending on the exposure to fraud and the actual losses incurred if fraud has occurred, my recommendation would be one of the former options. If the losses are negligible, it will still be necessary to terminate the responsible parties although it may be more costly for the company to pursue legal actions to reach a decision or settlement in the matter. If the losses are substantial, civil remedies will be recommended against all perpetrators. Only if the losses are extraordinary or the claim that the Felony Lane Gang is involved with the kickback scheme is substantiated should criminal action be considered. It is arguably necessary if there is gang involvement because of the potential for organized crime to be occurring such that State or federal attorneys might get to utilize the RICO Act in their prosecutions.

Utilizing Records to Obtain Evidence

The process of investigating stolen assets has a twofold purpose: “to determine the extent of embezzlement and… to gather evidence that can be used in interrogation to obtain a confession” (Albrecht et al, 2019, p. 247). If anyone working at the Teller CNB branch did in fact commit counterfeit check fraud, then an ideal conversion investigation would uncover discrepancies in his assets such that there would need to be an unknown source providing for his lifestyle, that being the money fraudulently deprived from CNB and its customers. The potential for workplace cultures and intra-organizational conduct to ideate “macro-level forces significantly altering the extent of dishonest behavior” in employees of the company provides reasoning for the necessity of obtaining records, as opposed to simply interviewing everyone at CNB and taking their words as truth (Houdek, 2020, p. 702).

Federal records like previous court case involvement could be useful, although the nature of the alleged kickback scheme upsets the relative importance of different types of federal records such that it would be most useful for fraud investigators to interact with the local U.S. Postal Service chapter for assistance with obtaining records as well as general investigative support. This is because “bribes and kickbacks… are often deposited into banks by sending them through the mail” (Albrecht et al, 2019, p. 249). This connection lends support to the argument that the Postal Service would be an invaluable partner in this investigation.

Albrecht et al (2019) provides a wealth of information on other resources for records as well (p. 249-254). State resources are both plentiful and potentially additive to the investigative experience in a variety of ways. For instance, the Departments of Motor Vehicles and Vital Statistics both maintain information that could identify potential areas of interest worth paying attention to when trying to uncover converted assets, like addresses and social security numbers. Local records like a marriage license may provide another person to assess and interview which could then paint a fuller image of the events as they occurred (if, for example, the conversion were occurring via deposits into a spouse’s account or investment portfolio). Likewise, real estate records obtained through the County Land Office or Tax Assessor’s Office could reveal similar conversion outcomes (in this case, the assets would be converted to newly acquired land).

Records of interest uncovered during the process of scouring public sources should be supplanted with information uncovered from private, typically online, sources as well. For instance, sites like accurint.com and netronline.com provide banker/banking information and public records (Albrecht et al, 2019, p. 254). Finally, online searches like Google queries should be utilized at the very least to assess whether any other relevant and accessible information exists. If the fraud is occurring, obtaining these kinds of records will be instrumental to providing the basis for that claim of occurrence and any punitive actions which might follow.

The Fraud Interview Strategy

The interview process is a vital aspect of a fraud examination. An important part of the interview process for investigators is to utilize known information as foundational tools to elicit the production of unknown information in interviewees. This can be facilitated by the development of profiles of the interviewees. To build a profile as it pertains to fraud examination, one could search for information on his assets, liabilities, and evaluations of their annual net worth (both outwardly ascertained and mathematically derived) over a significant span of their tenures at Colorado National Bank. These kinds of specific financial information coupled with a more individualized assessment of the tellers and managers as people developed through interaction with coworkers and clients would help to solidify a profile for the alleged perpetrators. Albrecht et al. (2019) recommend that as much information on both the offense and the interviewee as possible is gathered before conducting an interview (p. 272).

Generally speaking, interviewees should be arranged in relative order of potential culpability such that those least involved or least likely to be involved are interviewed first and those most greatly suspected of the fraud are interviewed last. A unique element of this situation is the anonymous tip predicating the fraud now examined. Because “the evidence came in through a tip from an identified source”, the source ought to be the first to be interviewed, if at all possible, with questions like: ‘What do you know?’ or ‘what is your motivation for making the report?’ (Association of Certified Fraud Examiners, 2016, p. 3.123). In the beginning of the interview process, some customers and most (if not all) employees of the Teller, CO bank branch should be considered potential interviewees. Information ascertained from the evidence-collecting process prior to the interview stage as well as any information gleaned from an interview with the anonymous source ought to be used to develop that potential list of interviewees into a structured, intentionally ordered list. Most of the initial interviews with those most likely to have little or nothing to do with the scheme ought to be met with informational questions to further develop an understanding and a foundation of information concerning “accounting control systems… documents… regarding business operations or systems…” as well as social information about other employees/suspects (Albrecht et al, 2019, p. 279). The tellers of the bank, including the head teller, as well as the assistant manager, will make up the tail-end of the ordered interview list since the conduct described in the anonymous letter most clearly lines up with methods most directly available to those serving in teller positions. Each of these interviews should begin without warning if possible. In fact, it would be incredibly beneficial to the fraud investigation for multiple examiners to interview singular interviewees at this stage at the same time, albeit separated. This can disincentivize the development of false backstories or alibis, clarifying what is and is not true with greater precision for the fraud examiners involved.

Finally, the primary fraud suspect should be interviewed last. If at all possible, this interview would benefit the greatest by being impromptu, rather than scheduled. All categories of interview questions will be present in this interview, although assessment questions to calibrate behavioral expectations for the interviewee and admission-seeking questions to ascertain guilt will be the most important to utilize in the interview. Albrecht et al (2019) suggests that incriminating documentation gathered in the investigative process leading to the ultimate interview can be used in the admission-seeking process, such as by “hand[ing] one or two documents to the respondent and ask[ing] for ‘comments’… in many cases, [they] will admit to incriminating conduct on the spot” (p. 290). If the investigation has uncovered adequate evidence to establish misconduct has occurred prior to the interview, it is within the right of the fraud examiner to directly, albeit carefully accuse the primary fraud suspect while refusing to positively engage with any denials of said accusation. Ideally, a verbal confession can be acquired and promptly substantiated by a written and signed confession. In the case of confession, it is incredibly important for the interviewer to question as meticulously as possible to ascertain rough estimates of stolen funds, the timeline for the fraud, whether it is still occurring, and who else is directly or indirectly involved in the fraud. A successful interview strategy will produce a wealth of additional evidence and in some cases, provide an immediate route to case closure through the procurement of a detailed confession.

The Fraud Report

Following the conclusion of the interview process, a fraud report must be created. Utilizing interview notes, summaries, and recordings, as well as the analyses of financial information developed during planning and investigation, one can compose a fraud report to be submitted once their investigation has come to an end. The fraud report will summarize all relevant information and give a recommendation for the company to follow. Albrecht et al (2019) states similarly that a fraud report “includes all findings, conclusions, recommendations, and corrective actions taken” (p. 301). If the investigation were to uncover evidence giving weight to the allegations in the anonymous letter, the fraud report would detail this in a factual, non-judgmental manner. A fraud report is a keystone of the fraud examination process because it summarizes and concludes the examination.

Conclusion

The importance of a proper investigative plan for any fraud examination where predication exists cannot be overstated. Umar et al (2017) found through copious research that “dysfunctional audit behavior has an adverse effect to detection fraud” (p. 111). Therefore, it is in the best interest of the company, its security, and its fraud examiners to adhere to a sound plan while investigating the allegations of fraud levied at employees working for the Teller CNB branch. Upon receipt of all pertinent sources of evidence and, if considered appropriate, the completion of all necessary interviews relating to the case, the truth of the matter ought to be elucidated such that further action can be taken and concerns can be determined to be unfounded or not.
