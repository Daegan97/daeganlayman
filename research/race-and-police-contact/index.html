Investigating a Potential Relationship between Race and Frequency of Police Contact

Daegan Layman
Graduate paper, Analytical Methods, M.S. Criminal Justice (CSU Global), 2022. 9 min read.

Abstract. A one-way ANOVA in SPSS on the 2008 Police-Public Contact Survey (Bureau of Justice Statistics, via ICPSR; 9,522 respondents) testing whether how often people have face-to-face contact with police differs by race. It found no significant difference, and the discussion is careful about what that does and doesn’t show.

The examination of potential relationships between race and police activity in its various forms proves a salient topic in the realm of statistics, where results can bolster and clarify arguments and interpretations conducive to a discussion that often becomes volatile in practice. The two variables examined in this secondary data analysis from the 32022 Codebook are V3, number of contacts with police in the last year, and RACEHISP, or a five-category variable for race of respondent.

The research question posed prior to statistical examination of a potential relationship between these variables is the following: Does one’s race have an effect on the frequency of police contact they might come to experience? The null hypothesis is that all racial categories experience the same amount of police contact. The hypothesis is that there are differences in amount of police contact one experiences based on one’s race. Research for this hypothesis will not be concerned with differences in quality of treatment during police contact based on race but rather differences in frequency.

Literature Review

Chenane et al. (2020) researched the relationships between traffic stops, race, and citizen attitudes concerning those stops (p. 731). Curious about whether negative interactions with police during traffic stops affect a citizen’s willingness to later seek help from police, Chenane and fellow researchers hypothesized that traffic stops perceived as illegitimate or unfair hurt civilian trust in policing institutions (Chenane et al, 2020, p. 721). The researchers conducted secondary data analysis on “data from the Police Public Contact Survey… sampl[ing]… all citizens… [from a sample of 62,280 subjects] who had face-to-face contacts with police” (Chenane et al, 2020, p. 724). They then used logistic regression analysis on the sample to confirm or deny their prediction that a relationship exists between traffic stop experiences and desire (or lack thereof) to contact police when in need of assistance. Most significantly, they found that “experiencing at least one traffic stop in the past year decreased the odds of seeking help from the police by approximately 67%” (Chenane et al, 2020, p. 728). They also found significant racial differences in observed relationships between seeking help from police and whether the individual received a ticket during their stop or what income bracket they belonged to, but no significant racial differences in perception of fairness during traffic stops.

The researchers concluded that their hypothesis should be accepted, observing an inverse relationship between experience with traffic stops and future voluntary interactions with police. They concede that the use of more recent data (sample data in the research study is from 2011) and the ambiguous temporal nature of the cross-sectional data they employ for the study leaves room for further development and refinement of the hypothesis, although they nonetheless conclude with the evidence they provide that consistent, respectful conduct during traffic stops will reduce the negative effect such stops have on individuals’ disposition towards the idea of reaching out to police when in need of help.

Ivanich and Warner (2019) examined data from “the Midwest Longitudinal Study of Homeless Adolescents (MLSHA) to examine the extent to which race shapes police contact” (p. 818). They sought specifically to determine whether non-white homeless youths were more, less, or equally likely to experience police contact in comparison to contact rates with white homeless youths. These hypotheses were labeled disproportionate minority contact, master status, and out-of-place, respectively. To determine an answer to these questions, the researchers examined interview responses collected by the MLSHA program, coding responses concerning harassment and arrest between interview periods as dichotomous ‘yes or no’ responses. These dependent variable values were then coupled with the independent variables, namely race/ethnicity, which was simplified and recorded dichotomously as ‘white or non-white’. Other independent variables included street exposure, risk behaviors, and prior criminal justice contact to better contextualize responses. Finally, control variables were gender, age, and number of interviews. Because of the nature of the MLSHA, GEE logistic regression was used to ascertain odds of contact (Ivanich and Warner, 2019, p. 828).

After analyzing the data, the researchers concluded that “non-White homeless youth have 1.728 times the odds of reporting police harassment (than White youth)” (Ivanich and Warner, 2019, p. 829). Likewise, they found that non-White homeless youth were more likely to be arrested as well. They concluded that they should accept their first hypothesis, the disproportionate minority contact model, based on the results of their analysis while recommending more robust surveying and sampling so that the conclusions can be more properly generalized to the national population of homeless youth. Finally, they recommend a deeper investigation of differences between race and police contact beyond the dichotomous white/non-white distinction.

Slocum and Wiley (2018) engaged in similar research, choosing to focus instead more globally on youth in general rather than homeless youth in particular. Like the previous researchers, Slocum and Wiley utilize data from longitudinal surveys to test a hypothesis of “whether there is variability in the observed relationships [between police and youth] by race or ethnicity” (Slocum and Wiley, 2018, p. 409). Their data comes from surveys of youth participating in the National Evaluation of the Gang Resistance Education and Training program, from a generous sample of over 3,000 (compared to the former study’s sub-500 sample size). While there were numerous dependent variables in the study, they all concern how the interviewee perceptions on police, crime, and their own delinquency measured incrementally from negative to positive. The key independent variable of the study then is police contact, measured in frequency and satisfaction with the encounter. The researchers state they control for “age, sex, nativity, household structure, parental education, and residential mobility” (Slocum and Wiley, 2018, p. 414). Using structural equation modeling and maximum likelihood estimation, the researchers found a suitable model to run their analyses twice: once with only “basic demographic variables… [and] a full model with all variables” (Slocum and Wiley, 2018, p. 416).

The researchers found that dissatisfaction with treatment from police was predictive of negative outcomes for those surveyed individuals. Overall, the researchers concluded that “negatively perceived police-initiated encounters are associated with a variety of detrimental outcomes” regardless of race (Slocum and Wiley, 2018, p. 422). They recommend that diversion programs and an emphasis on positive police-initiated encounters as vital steps toward the construction of a system that can police citizens without negatively affecting their actions and perceptions as they relate to the police in the future.

There are a variety of results across studies related to this study’s hypothesis, that there are differences in amount of police contact one experiences based on one’s race. This analysis will contribute to the wider examination of police-race relations by determining whether a relationship exists between frequency of contact and one’s race using sample data from the Inter-University Consortium for Political and Social Research’s 2008 police-public contact survey. Using data from the RACEHISP and V3 variables, one can statistically analyze whether a relationship exists between the two.

Method

The independent variable in this study is race. In the police-public contact survey, this RACEHISP variable is divided into five categories: 1 – White, 2 – Black, 3 – Hispanic, 4 – Other, and 5 – Two or more races. The dependent variable is frequency of police contact during the last year, classified as V3 in the survey. It is a numerical variable with distinct values for refusal, lack of knowledge, and missing values.

The data collected to inform these variables was collected as part of the National Crime Victimization Survey, an annual survey that in 2008, when the data used in this study was collected, surveyed 72,566 individuals, interviewing 57,978 of them (ICPSR, 2008, p. 6). Answers to these surveys and follow-up interviews produced data which was organized into each of the 127 variables detailed in the 2008 police-public contact survey. The United States Bureau of the Census sponsored and collected the data to “provide[] detailed information on the nature and characteristics of face-to-face contacts between police and the public” (ICPSR, 2008, p. 5). In the summary data description, the unit of analysis is individuals, and the universe is all United States persons over the age of 12 (ICPSR, 2008, p. 5)

A one-way analysis of variance test will be the statistical method utilized to investigate the hypothesis of this study. It is the appropriate method because the relationship being examined is between a categorical variable comprised of five groupings and a dependent numerical variable, number of face-to-face police contacts during the past year. A post-hoc test is not included in the following statistical graphics because of the high p-value or lack of significance. Frankfort-Nachmias et al (2021) state that “a larger F obtained statistic means that there is more between-group variance than within-group variance, increasing the chances of rejecting our null hypothesis” (p. 751). Appendix E (p. 921) shows the distribution of F along various degrees of freedom. The F statistic calculated in this ANOVA analysis is extremely low in comparison to an F statistic that could hint at a statistically significant relationship. Likewise, the p-value or significance value is 0.764, substantially higher than the selected alpha-value of 0.05 and therefore one must conclude that there is not a statistically significant difference between groups. With respect to the hypothesis, this means that the data fails to give cause for rejection of the null hypothesis that all racial categories experience the same amount of police contact.

Analysis and Findings

Discussion

The result of the one-way analysis of variance test between the independent categorical RACEHISP variable and the numerical V3 frequency of police contact variable revealed that there was not a significant difference between the means of police contact experienced over a year-long period in each racial category. This nonsignificant result means that the null hypothesis cannot be rejected. Despite this, this study’s sole purpose to investigate whether there existed a relationship between race and frequency of police contact was narrowly focused; further investigation of any potential relationships between RACE-type variables and the numerous police contact-type variables (V-class) could produce meaningful and perhaps even significant results. Initially, the ANOVA analysis for this study was conducted between the RACE and V3 variables which proved difficult because of how many categories RACE had and the inconsistency of population size between those categories. Regardless, it could prove fruitful to further investigate more specific categories of race against various variables to continue unearthing potential relationships between race and police conduct. This point is especially relevant to this study, which only focused on frequency of contact, rather than quality of contact. Regardless of a nonsignificant difference in frequency of contact between racial categories, there could still exist discrepancies in quality of treatment while in contact with police between racial categories that examination of potential relationships between other variables could possibly discover.

Finally, statistics is an ever evolving and forever imperfect science, like the rest of them. Consider the concern “that ES [effect size] measures for ANOVA models are often defined in a somewhat ad hoc manner… definition-by-example approach” (Marsman et al., 2019, p. 351). The lodger of the complaint goes on in their 2019 mathematical research article to show a “theory on squared multiple correlation coefficients provid[ing] a useful framework for deriving ES measures for the ANOVA model”, demonstrating that statistical tests are susceptible to uncomfortable margins of error that can continue to be partially remedied by mathematical addendums and evolutions of existing frameworks and models (Marsman et al., 2019, p. 369).

The failure to reject the null hypothesis in this study is not a failure of the study and still provides valuable insight into the relationships (or lack thereof) between certain variables in the police-public contact survey dataset.
